Chapter 1a — Marketing Orientations & Fundamentals (Kotler Ch.1, first half)

Source decks: Kotler Ch.1 slides 1-12. Source lectures: Lec 1, Lec 2 (parts of Lec 3-2 for relationship marketing). All claims below cite [Slide ...] or [Lec N @ mm:ss].

In one sentence

Marketing is everything a company does to understand a customer first and then design the product, price, distribution and communication around that customer. Selling is pushing a product that already exists. Marketing is the opposite: pulling a product into existence because customers told you what they need. [Slide: Kotler Ch.1 — Definition of Marketing]

In plain language

Imagine you want to open a bakery. Two different mindsets:

  • The selling mindset — bake whatever bread you like, then chase people down the street shouting about how fresh it is. If sales are slow, shout louder or drop the price.
  • The marketing mindset — walk around the neighbourhood first. Ask: who lives here? Are they diabetic? Do they want low-carb? Do they want french bread or baladi? What do they pay now? When do they buy? Then design the bakery around what you learned.

This is called outside-in thinking (start from the market) versus inside-out thinking (start from the factory). Dr. Alaa hammers the point: marketing starts before production, selling starts after production [Lec 1 @ 1:01:30].

Needs vs wants vs demand. A need is a basic requirement — food, shelter, transport, status. A want is a specific desire shaped by culture and personality — shawarma vs. pizza, Toyota vs. BMW. Demand is a want backed by the ability to pay. Marketing doesn't invent needs; it reshapes wants and converts them into demand.

Core concepts

1.2 The five marketing management orientations

Companies have historically taken one of five stances toward the market. These stances evolved as markets matured: Production → Product (quality) → Selling → Marketing → Societal. [Slide: Kotler Ch.1 s.6-10] The first three are inside-out (factory-first); the last two are outside-in (customer-first).

Orientation Core belief Real-world icon
Production Make it cheap and available. If you can produce it, it will sell. Used when demand outstrips supply. Henry Ford's Model T; Egyptian Nasr-era factories — Ideal, Atlas, Harbeya refrigerators, Fajr stoves.
Product (Quality) Make it better. Consumers will pay more for higher quality, performance, features. Constant improvement. Post-war Japan — Toyota, Sony, Honda. Rebuilt through obsession with quality.
Selling The market is saturated; the customer won't buy unless we shout. Invest in promotion, discounts, payment facilities. Insurance salespeople, encyclopedia door-knockers, real-estate cold-calls.
Marketing Start with the customer's needs; design everything around them. Build a long-term relationship. Apple, Starbucks, IKEA — companies obsessed with understanding the user's life.
Societal Balance customer wants with society's long-term welfare: environment, health, vulnerable groups. Patagonia (environment), Dove (body image), Tesla (sustainability).

Why do you need all five? Because different markets and different eras demand different stances. Egypt in 1960 had a production-concept market (demand > supply). Egypt in 2026 has a marketing-concept market (consumer is overwhelmed by choice). A single company can evolve through stages as its industry matures.

Important slide bullet Dr. Alaa emphasised: the first three orientations (Production, Product, Selling) do NOT support long-term customer relationships — they end up creating dissatisfaction and switching. Only Marketing and Societal build loyalty. [Slide: Kotler Ch.1 s.6-10]

1.3 Relationship marketing and one-to-one marketing

Relationship marketing means treating every customer interaction as one point in a long-term relationship, not a standalone transaction. One-to-one marketing is the extreme version: technology lets you tailor the product and the message to a single individual. [Slide: Kotler Ch.1 s.12]

The old Kodak slogan, "Press the button, we do the rest" — that's transactional. The customer gives money, gets a photo, goes home. Contrast it with Netflix: every show you watch teaches the system what to recommend next. Each interaction deepens the relationship and makes leaving the platform more painful. That is relationship marketing.

One-to-one marketing is when your product line has effectively one unit per customer. A bride gets her wedding car painted with her name; a private-banking client has an investment portfolio no one else in the world owns; a custom music playlist is generated for you alone. It's only possible because technology made it cheap to customise at scale (mass customisation).

Chapter 1b — Strategic Marketing Management (Kerin Ch.1 + Strategic Pilot deck)

Source decks: Kerin Ch.1 slides + Strategic Marketing Pilot deck (Basic Concepts s.2-3, Strategic Pilot s.5-37). Source lectures: Lec 1 (mostly), Lec 2 (mostly). All claims below cite [Slide ...] or [Lec N @ mm:ss].

In one sentence

Strategic marketing management is the five-step cycle a company runs (and re-runs as the environment changes) to decide what business it is in, what opportunities to pursue, which products to send to which markets, how to fund the plan, and how to recover when things go wrong. [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot]

In plain language

The strategic-marketing layer sits above day-to-day marketing operations. While a campaign manager is choosing today's ad copy, the strategic marketer is asking: which business are we even in?, which opportunities are worth pursuing for the next 5–10 years?, which products go to which markets?, how is the plan funded?, and what do we do when the environment turns against us?

The 8 reasons listed on Basic Concepts slide 2 (slide title: "Why studying Strategic Marketing Management?" — see next section) explain why strategic marketing is a distinct discipline. The five processes (§2.1) are the cycle every firm runs. SWOT (§2.3) is the diagnostic that drives Process 2 (opportunity identification). The Ansoff matrix (§2.4) is the product-market grid for Process 3 (strategy formulation). The 4Ps overview (§2.5) is the tactical lever-set inside Process 3 — and the deep dives on each P live in the four new-chapter tabs (Ch.8 Product, Ch.10 Pricing, Ch.11 Promotion, Ch.14 Place). Budgeting and the marketing audit (§2.6) close the cycle.

Core concepts

2.1 Processes in Strategic Marketing Management

Before the processes, three slide-grounded preludes:

Why studying Strategic Marketing Management? — the 8 reasons on slide 2 [Slide: Basic Concepts s.2]:

  1. Changes in market structure — new entrants, consolidations, deregulation, e-commerce reshaping how a market is organised.
  2. Competition — globalisation means the threat is no longer just the shop next door; it's a Chinese or Turkish substitute landed at half your price.
  3. Cultural dynamics — values, family roles, religious adherence, gender norms shift over time. What sold in 2000 doesn't always sell now.
  4. Micro & Macro factors — micro: customers, suppliers, intermediaries, competitors. Macro: economy, demographics, technology, politics, law.
  5. Impact of technology — smartphones, AI, e-payments, social media advertising. Technology destroys old channels (Kodak film) and creates new ones (Netflix, ride-hailing).
  6. Marginal Propensity to Save / Consume — when income rises, do people save (luxury goods slow down) or consume (luxury goods boom)? Marginal propensity to consume applies most to basic goods; marginal propensity to save applies more to luxury goods.
  7. Changes in buyers' behaviour — younger generations buy online, read reviews, switch brands faster, ignore TV ads.
  8. Heterogeneity (تنوع المنتجات) — markets fragment into more sub-segments every year. There is no average customer any more.

The marketing manager's evolved role: from tactical executor (printing brochures, running campaigns, organising trade shows) to strategy maker [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.5-6]. Dr. Alaa's verbatim:

From being only an implementer ... to being a maker of market organization. هو بقى الميكر، هو اللي بيصنع الاستراتيجي للشركة

"They are now the maker, the one who builds the strategy for the company." [Lec 2 @ 42:17-42:31]

Expanded responsibilities: - Charting the direction of the organisation — deciding which markets and products the company should pursue 5–10 years out. - Contributing to decisions that create and sustain a competitive advantage — pricing, branding, positioning calls that affect long-term performance. - Making strategic decisions, not just operational ones — sitting at the executive table.

Sizing the market strategically — the four levels [Slide: Basic Concepts s.3]:

  1. Market Potential — the absolute ceiling. How many units could conceivably be sold under ideal conditions to every possible buyer? (Every adult in Egypt is a potential mobile-phone buyer.)
  2. Industry Sales Forecast — what the entire industry is expected to actually sell next period (less than market potential).
  3. Company Sales Forecast — what your firm expects to sell = market share × industry sales.
  4. Unrealized Potential — the gap between Market Potential and your Company Sales. This is the white space — the room to grow.

The strategic mindset is to constantly ask: where is my unrealized potential, and which marketing actions can convert it? A complacent firm forecasts company sales and stops there. A strategic firm forecasts industry sales, sees the gap, and plans how to close it.

The 5 processes — deck-verbatim wording [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.7] (memorise these forms — Dr. Alaa tests using this exact phrasing):

  1. Defining the organization's business, mission, and goals [Slide s.8 — Process One]
  2. Identifying and framing organizational growth opportunities [Slide s.15 — Process Two]
  3. Formulating product-market strategies [Slide s.23 — Process Three]
  4. Budgeting marketing, financial, and production resources [Slide s.33 — Process Four]
  5. Developing reformulation and recovery strategies [Slide s.35 — Process Five]

Plain-language gloss (what each process actually does — use this to understand, but quote the verbatim list above on the exam):

  1. Define the business, mission, and goals. What are we here for? Who do we serve? Where do we draw the line?
  2. Identify and frame growth opportunities. What's happening in the environment? Which opportunities fit our strengths? (SWOT lives here.)
  3. Formulate product-market strategies. Ansoff's grid: penetration, market development, product development, diversification. Plus the Marketing Mix (4Ps).
  4. Budget marketing, financial, and production resources. Translate the plan into money — operating budget (income statement) and financial budget (cash flow). Note the 3 resource categories — same Production / Financial / Marketing trio as the Business Goals slide (s.13-14).
  5. Develop reformulation and recovery strategies. When the environment turns — war, pandemic, currency shock — rework the plan.

"First you gather information, then you do SWOT — strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats." [Lec 2 @ 33:38]

2.2 Process One: Defining the Organization's Business, Mission, and Goals

[Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.8 — Process One anchor]

Process One asks: who are we, what are we here for, and what does success look like? Before any scanning or strategy can begin, the organisation must define its business from the market's perspective, crystallise that into a mission statement, and convert the mission into concrete, measurable goals across three categories.

Define your business from the market (what the customer's need is), not from the product (what you happen to produce). This one shift protects you from going obsolete when technology changes the product.

Kodak defined itself as "we make film." When digital photography arrived, Kodak died. If Kodak had defined itself as "we capture memories," it would have embraced digital cameras instead of fighting them.

Dr. Alaa's four canonical market-perspective redefinitions:

Company Product perspective (narrow) Market perspective (wide)
Toyota We make cars. We provide transportation solutions — land, sea, air; trucks, buses, vans, forklifts.
Microsoft (Bill Gates) We sell software. "We are not producing products, we are solving our customers' problems." This is literally where the word "solutions" entered the software industry.
Disney We run amusement parks / make films. We provide entertainment.
IKEA We sell furniture. "Home dress" — every single thing that goes into making a home a home: furniture, candles, bedsheets, even chlorine.

Why this matters: if you call yourself "a sweets shop" (El-Abd, El-Orouba), you'll lose when a café opens next door that also sells nuts, sweets, coffee. But if you define yourself as an entertainment venue, the café is a natural extension, not a threat.

Business definition answers three questions: (1) Who are our customers? (2) What needs do we satisfy? (3) With what means / technology?

The mission statement then formalises the business definition into a short public statement — capturing purpose, customers, products, markets, philosophy, technology. It is revisable as the business evolves.

Why a mission statement matters — 4 specific benefits [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.12]:

  1. Crystallizes management's vision of the organisation's long-term direction and character. Forces leadership to put a stake in the ground.
  2. Provides guidance in identifying, pursuing, and evaluating market and product opportunities. When a new opportunity arises, the mission tells you whether it fits.
  3. Inspires and challenges employees to do those things valued by the organisation and its customers. A good mission rallies the team; a generic mission inspires nobody.
  4. Provides direction for setting business goals or objectives. The mission tells you what to measure; the goals tell you whether you're hitting it.

Business goals — the three categories

Goals (or objectives) convert the organisation's mission into tangible actions and results that are to be achieved, often within a specified time frame. [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.13]

The deck names three major categories of goals: [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.13-14]

CategoryWhat the slide says
Production Goals Apply to the use of manufacturing and service capacity and to product and service quality.
Financial Goals Focus on return on investment, return on sales, profit, cash flow, and shareholder wealth.
Marketing Goals Market share · marketing productivity · sales volume · profit · customer satisfaction · customer value creation

Exam tip: The most tested distinction is Financial vs Marketing. A question asking "what kind of goal is 'increase market share'?" → Marketing goal. "Increase ROI?" → Financial goal. Production goals are rarely the answer choice but know the definition.

2.3 Process Two: Identifying and Framing Organizational Growth Opportunities

[Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.15 — Process Two anchor]

Process Two asks: what real growth opportunities exist for us, and which of them actually fit who we are? After the mission and goals are set (Process One), the organisation must scan its environment, map its own strengths, and frame only those opportunities where its capabilities genuinely match what the market needs. This is where the analytical work begins — before any strategy is formulated.

A real growth opportunity exists only where your internal strengths (distinctive competencies) match the external environment's needs (success requirements of the market).

Environmental opportunities have three typical sources: (1) unmet or changing customer needs, (2) unsatisfied buyer groups, (3) new means or technology for delivering value. [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.16]

Distinctive Competency — verbatim deck definition [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.17]:
"describes an organization's unique strengths or qualities including: Skills, Technologies, Resources …that distinguish it from other organizations."

(Use to understand: it's what you do best — the capabilities competitors can't easily replicate. But quote the verbatim definition above on the exam.) [Slide: Kerin Ch.1 s.18]

Success Requirements — verbatim deck definition [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.18]:
"are basic tasks that an organization must perform in a market or industry to compete successfully."

Critical conditional — verbatim [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.18]:
"If what must be done is inconsistent with what can be done to capitalize on an environmental opportunity, an organizational growth opportunity will fail to materialize."

(Use to understand: if your distinctive competency doesn't cover the market's success requirements, the opportunity is not yours.)

SWOT analysis — the diagnostic framework

SWOT — verbatim deck definition [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.19]:
"A formal framework for identifying and framing organizational growth opportunities."

An organizational growth opportunity results from [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.20]:
"a good fit between an organization's INTERNAL CAPABILITIES (Strengths & Weaknesses) and its EXTERNAL ENVIRONMENT (Opportunities & Threats)."

SWOT arranges the diagnostic as a 2×2 [Slide: Kerin Ch.1 s.20]:

Favourable Unfavourable
Internal (you) Strengths — brand, money, location, technology Weaknesses — outdated facilities, weak talent
External (world) Opportunities — new segments, tech shifts, crises creating demand Threats — currency shocks, war, substitute products

Verbatim slide definitions — quote these exactly on the exam [Slide: Kerin Ch.1 s.21]:

ElementDeck-verbatim definition
Strength What the organization is good at doing or a characteristic that gives it an important capability.
Weakness What an organization lacks or does poorly relative to competitors.
Opportunities Developments or conditions in the environment that have favorable implications for the organization.
Threats Pose dangers to the organization.

Dr. Alaa was emphatic:

ده framework اللي بيتعامل لازم أي تحطيط استراتيجي على المدى طويل

"This framework MUST be used in any long-term strategic planning." [Lec 2 @ 1:45:59]

Three diagnostic questions to ask once SWOT is on the page [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.22]:

  1. Which internal strengths represent distinctive competencies? Deck-verbatim: "Do these strengths compare favorably with what are believed to be market or industry success requirements?" [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.22] Not every "strength" is a real differentiator — if a strength is widely shared by competitors, it isn't distinctive.
  2. Which internal weaknesses disqualify the organisation from pursuing certain opportunities? Some weaknesses are deal-breakers — no money for capex, no licence, no distribution reach in the target geography. These rule whole opportunity classes out of the picture early.
  3. Does a pattern emerge from the SWOT? The most useful insight is rarely a single S, W, O, or T item — it's the cross-pairing. A Strength matched to an Opportunity = offensive move. A Weakness matched to a Threat = existential risk.

The compound real-estate opportunity [Lec 1 @ 41:30]: before 2000, Egypt had only traditional apartment buildings. Gulf-based developers (Emaar, SODIC, Dajla, Mountain View) saw a gap — Egyptians with rising income who wanted gated security, parking, and services. They built compounds. Result: Egyptians now pay double the per-m² price for amenities the old buildings never offered. That's an environmental opportunity converted into an organisational one — but only because the developers had the distinctive competency (capital, construction tech, imported compound-design know-how).

COVID pivot — BYD making masks [Lec 2 @ 1:48:21]: a Chinese car maker stopped car production and pivoted its factory to masks. The environmental opportunity (pandemic mask shortage) matched BYD's distinctive competency (automated assembly line, supply chain speed). Nokia, similarly, was originally a forestry / wood-products company before it pivoted to mobile.

Crocs vs Skechers [Lec 1 @ 43:43]: Crocs had a distinctive competency in comfort material. Skechers copied and surpassed them. This is why a company needs a sustainable competitive advantage — something competitors can't easily replicate. Apple's AirPods made wireless the default; no customer accepts wired headphones anymore [Lec 1 @ 2:23:00]. That's sustainable.

2.4 Process Three: Formulating Product-Market Strategies

[Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.23 — Process Three anchor]

Process Three asks: which markets should we enter, and with which products? Having identified a real opportunity (Process Two), the organisation now chooses a growth trajectory using the Ansoff Matrix, then designs the Marketing Mix (4Ps) to execute it. Both tools belong to this process — Ansoff sets the direction, the 4Ps provide the levers.

The Ansoff Matrix — choosing a growth trajectory

Ansoff's 2×2 grid (Existing vs New products, Existing vs New markets) gives four growth strategies: Market Penetration, Market Development, Product Development, and Diversification — in increasing order of risk.

Existing products New products
Existing markets Market Penetration — sell more of what you already have to customers you already reach. Product Development — create new offerings for your existing markets.
New markets Market Development — introduce existing offerings to markets you don't yet serve. Diversification — new product, new market. High-risk strategy.

Verbatim deck definitions — quote these exactly on the exam [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.25–29]:

StrategyDeck-verbatim definition
Market Penetration"Seeking a larger market share in a market in which organization already has an offering."
Market Development"Introducing its existing offerings to markets other than those that the organization is currently serving."

Tactics per strategy — verbatim from deck:

  • Market Penetration [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.25]: (1) Attempts to increase present buyer's usage or consumption rates of the offering; (2) Attracting buyers of competing offerings; (3) Stimulating product trial among potential consumers.
  • Market Development [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.26] — reaching new markets requires: (1) Carefully considering competitor strengths and weaknesses and competitor retaliation potential; (2) Modification of the basic offering; (3) Different distribution outlets; (4) Change in sales effort and advertising. Internationally: the four modes [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.27] — Exporting, Licensing, Joint Venture or Strategic Alliance, Direct Investment.
  • Product Development [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.28] — three modes: Product Innovation – develop totally new offerings; Product Augmentation – enhance the value to customers of existing offerings; Product Line Extension – broaden the existing line of offerings by adding different sizes, forms, flavors, etc.
  • Diversification [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.29]: high-risk because both the offering and market served are new to the organization. Often done by acquisition.

2.5 The Marketing Mix — 4Ps overview (tactical layer within Process Three, bridge to Ch.8/10/11/14)

Once you've chosen a growth trajectory via Ansoff (§2.4), the 4Ps are the four levers you actually pull to execute that strategy. They are always designed around the customer, who sits at the centre [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.4]:

2.6 Processes Four and Five: Budgeting, Reformulation, and the Marketing Audit

Process Four: Budgeting Marketing, Financial, and Production Resources [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.33 — Process Four anchor]

Process Four translates the chosen strategy into money. The organisation allocates its three resource categories — Marketing, Financial, and Production — in a formal budget (note: same trio as the Business Goals categories in §2.2).

Budget (textbook verbatim, slide 33): "a formal, quantitative expression of an organisation's planning and strategy initiatives expressed in financial terms." A well-prepared budget meshes and balances the firm's Financial, Production, and Marketing resources so that overall organisational goals or objectives are attained [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.33]. Two components [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.34]:

  • Operating Budget (also referred to as a pro forma Income Statement) — focuses on an organization's income statement.
  • Financial Budget — focuses on the effect that the operating budget and other initiatives will have on the organization's cash position.

Process Five: Developing Reformulation and Recovery Strategies [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.35 — Process Five anchor]

Process Five asks: when the environment changes drastically, how do we rework the plan? Triggers include currency shocks, war, a pandemic, or a disruptive competitor. The output is either a reformulation (a reworked strategy for the new reality) or a recovery plan (getting back on track after a crisis). The Marketing Audit is the diagnostic tool that feeds Process Five — it tells you what needs reformulating.

The Marketing Audit — verbatim deck definition [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.36]: "Comprehensive, systematic, independent, and periodic examination of a company's marketing environment, objectives, strategies, and activities to recommend a plan of action to improve the company's marketing performance."

It answers two questions — memorise these exactly:

  1. Are we doing the right things? (Is our strategy aimed at the right markets, customers, value propositions?)
  2. Are we doing things right? (Are we executing the strategy efficiently — budgets, processes, channels?)

Both questions are necessary: a company can execute a flawed strategy brilliantly, or execute a brilliant strategy badly — either way the outcome suffers.

The Marketing Plan — slide 37's exact definition: "A formal written document that describes the context and scope of an organisation's marketing effort to achieve defined goals or objectives within a specified future time period." [Slide: Strategic Marketing Pilot s.37]

Two characteristics: - Focus — the plan can be written for a whole business, a single product, or a specific brand. Each level has its own document. - Time dimension — short-run plans typically cover one year (the annual marketing plan), long-run plans span multiple years (3–5 years for strategic plans).

How the plan relates to everything else in this course: the plan is the output of all five processes from §2.1. Process 1 (mission/goals) goes in the front. Process 2 (SWOT/opportunities) shapes the strategy section. Process 3 (Ansoff + 4Ps) produces the action plan. Process 4 (budget) gives it numbers. Process 5 (recovery) is the contingency section. The marketing audit then loops back to evaluate execution.

Chapter 7 — Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning (Kotler Ch.7)

Source deck: Kotler Ch.7 slides 1-33. Source lectures: Lec 3-1, Lec 3-2. All claims below cite [Slide ...] or [Lec N @ mm:ss].

Dr. Alaa called this "the central most important topic" of the course [Lec 3-1 @ 15:26] — proven on midterm (35% of all questions) and likely the heaviest topic on the final's STP question.

In one sentence

STP is the four-step customer-driven marketing-strategy engine: Segment the market into groups, Target the ones worth pursuing, Differentiate your offering, and Position it in the customer's mind. [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.2]

In plain language

You almost never sell to "everyone." Even Coca-Cola — the most universal product on Earth — runs different ads for different audiences and offers different sizes, flavours and packaging by segment. The STP engine forces you to (1) see the market as a collection of distinct groups rather than one undifferentiated mass (Segmentation), (2) choose which groups you can serve profitably (Targeting), (3) change your offer so it visibly fits the chosen group better than competitors (Differentiation), and (4) plant that difference in the customer's mind so they remember and prefer you (Positioning).

The order matters: S → T → D → P. Skip a step and the next one breaks. Skip Segmentation and you can't Target. Skip Differentiation and your Positioning is empty.

Core concepts

3.0 Four core definitions (deck-verbatim — quote these on the exam)

[Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.3–4]

  • Market segmentation — "is the process that companies use to divide large heterogeneous markets into small markets that can be reached more efficiently and effectively with products and services that match their unique needs."
  • Market targeting — "(targeting) is the process of evaluating each market segment's attractiveness and selecting one or more segments to enter."
  • Differentiation — "involves actually differentiating the market offering to create superior customer value."
  • Positioning — "consists of arranging for a market offering to occupy a clear, distinctive, and desirable place relative to competing products in the minds of target consumers."

Use to understand (not to quote on exam): Segmentation = carve the market into slices. Targeting = pick which slices to pursue. Differentiation = change your offer to fit the chosen slice better than rivals. Positioning = make that difference live in the customer's mind.

Figure 7.1 — Designing a Customer-Driven Marketing Strategy [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.5]: Two-stage flow — Select customers to serve (Segmentation → Targeting) feeds into a red centre box "Create value for targeted customers", which connects to Decide on a value proposition (Differentiation → Positioning). The four steps in order are: Segmentation → Targeting → Differentiation → Positioning.

3.2 Using multiple segmentation bases together

Slide 13's exact wording: "Multiple segmentation is used to identify smaller, better-defined target groups." [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.13]

Plain language: the more bases you combine, the smaller and sharper the segment becomes. Geographic + Demographic + Behavioural = "30–45-year-old women living in upper-Cairo, single-mum life-cycle stage, regular users of organic baby food, willing to pay premium prices." That's a far more actionable segment than just "women" or just "upper-Cairo residents." Marketers call this multivariate segmentation or geodemographic segmentation when geography + demographics combine. The trade-off: each extra base shrinks the segment size, so you have to balance precision against substantiality (one of the 5 effective-segmentation requirements below).

3.4 Segmenting international markets

When you slice across countries rather than within one country, the variables shift again. Slide 15 names four factors [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.15]:

Deck-verbatim list [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.15]:

  1. Geographic location
  2. Economic factors
  3. Political-legal factors
  4. Cultural factors

Use to understand (not to quote on exam):

  1. Geographic location — which region, continent, or climate zone the country sits in. Proximity shapes logistics costs, trade blocs, and consumer behaviour. Middle East, North Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa, South-East Asia each have distinct market profiles.
  2. Economic factors — the strength and structure of each country's economy (developed vs. developing, GDP per capita, currency stability, household disposable income). Premium cars fit high-income countries; sachet detergent fits low-income markets.
  3. Political-legal factors — political stability, regulatory environment, tax regimes, import/export rules, IP enforcement. A country with unstable politics or sudden tariff swings is a riskier segment even if its economics look attractive.
  4. Cultural factors — language, religion, social norms, values. Dr. Alaa highlighted religion most — what's halal/haram, what may be advertised, dress codes, dietary restrictions, gender-mix in public spaces. Beef burgers don't sell in India; pork doesn't sell in the Gulf; alcohol advertising is illegal in Saudi Arabia.

3.5 Inter-market segmentation

Easy to confuse with international segmentation, but it's the opposite move. Slide 16's exact wording: "Inter-market segmentation divides consumers into groups with similar needs and buying behaviors even though they are located in different countries." [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.16]

Plain language: instead of treating "Egypt" and "Germany" as two separate markets and tailoring to each, you find the same kind of customer in both countries and treat them as one global segment. Classic example: gamers. A 20-year-old PlayStation gamer in Cairo wants the same console, the same games, the same headset as a 20-year-old gamer in Berlin or Tokyo. Sony can sell the same product to all of them with the same global marketing because the segment travels across borders.

Other classic inter-market segments: luxury-watch buyers (a Rolex buyer in Dubai and a Rolex buyer in New York behave the same way), Apple iPhone power-users, Marlboro smokers, business-class air travellers. Lexus deliberately targets wealthy consumers in many countries — that's an inter-market play.

Quick distinction to memorise: - International segmentation = country-by-country. Each country = one segment with its own economic/political/cultural profile. You tailor to each country. - Inter-market segmentation = customer-type across countries. One segment, many countries. You treat the cross-border tribe as one market.

3.5b Requirements for effective segmentation

Deck-verbatim intro [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.17]: "To be useful, market segments must be:"

  • Measurable
  • Accessible
  • Substantial
  • Differentiable
  • Actionable

Use to understand (not to quote on exam):

  1. Measurable — you can size the segment (how many buyers, how much they spend).
  2. Accessible — you can reach them via a channel (media, sales force, distributor).
  3. Substantial — the segment is large enough to be profitable.
  4. Differentiable — the segments respond differently to different marketing mixes; if two segments respond identically, they're really one segment.
  5. Actionable — you can design an effective programme to attract and serve the segment.

3.6 What is a "target market"?

Once you've sliced the market into segments, you don't serve every segment — you pick the ones you'll actually go after. Slide 18's exact wording: "A target market consists of a set of buyers who share common needs or characteristics that the company decides to serve." [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.18]

Three things to notice in that definition: - "A set of buyers" — not the whole market, just a chosen group. - "Share common needs or characteristics" — the segment must be cohesive; what unites them must be more important than what divides them. - "The company decides to serve" — targeting is an active choice. You're saying yes to one segment and no to others, and the no's are just as important.

3.7 Evaluating market segments — the 3 criteria

Before you pick which segment(s) to chase, you have to evaluate each one. Slide 19 lists three criteria — every segment must be judged against all three [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.19]:

  1. Segment size and growth — is the segment big enough today, and is it growing? A small but fast-growing segment can be more attractive than a large but stagnant one.
  2. Segment structural attractiveness — how brutal is competition inside the segment? Borrow Porter's logic: are there many strong rivals? Cheap substitutes? Easy entry? A segment that looks big and growing can still be unattractive if a price war is dragging margins to zero.
  3. Company objectives and resources — does this segment fit your strategy and your capability? Only pursue segments where your distinctive competency matches the segment's success requirements (the same logic from Ch.1b §2.3).

A segment is "go" only when the answer to all three criteria is favourable.

3.8 Targeting — the 4 strategies

Figure 7.2 — Marketing Targeting Strategies [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.20]: A left-to-right spectrum with Targeting broadly on the left and Targeting narrowly on the right. Order: Undifferentiated (mass) → Differentiated (segmented) → Concentrated (niche) → Micromarketing (local or individual).

Deck-verbatim definitions [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.21–24] — quote these on the exam:

  • Undifferentiated marketing — "targets the whole market with one offer. Mass marketing. Focuses on common needs rather than what's different."
  • Differentiated marketing — "targets several different market segments and designs separate offers for each. Goal is to achieve higher sales and a stronger position. More expensive than undifferentiated marketing."
  • Concentrated marketing — "targets a large share of one or a few smaller segments or niches. Limited company resources. Knowledge of the market. More effective and efficient."
  • Micromarketing — "is the practice of tailoring products and marketing programs to suit the tastes of specific individuals and locations. Local marketing. Individual marketing."

Sub-types of Micromarketing (s.25–26):

  • Local marketing [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.25] — "involves tailoring brands and promotion to the needs and wants of local customer groups. Cities. Neighborhoods. Stores."
  • Individual marketing [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.26] — "involves tailoring products and marketing programs to the needs and preferences of individual customers. Also known as: One-to-one marketing. Customized marketing or mass customization. Markets-of-one marketing."

Use to understand (not to quote on exam) — strategy table:

Strategy Logic Example Dr. Alaa used
Undifferentiated (mass) One offer for the whole market. Works when the product has low variability. Water, Pepsi, plain biscuits [Lec 3-2 @ 36:04].

3.9 Choosing your targeting strategy — the 5 factors

Once you've evaluated the segments and picked a candidate, the next decision is which of the four targeting strategies to apply. Slide 27 names five factors that drive that choice [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.27 ; Lec 3-2 @ 35:06]:

  1. Company resources — small / cash-constrained → concentrated (niche). Large firms with deep pockets can afford differentiated or undifferentiated. Dr. Alaa: "limited company resource" [Lec 3-2 @ 18:11].
  2. Product variability — commodity-like products (water, sugar, plain biscuits) are uniform → undifferentiated. Highly variable products (phones, cars, cosmetics, fashion) → differentiated.
  3. Product life-cycle stage — at introduction, undifferentiated single offer is best (one message, build category awareness). As the market matures, the firm shifts to differentiated.
  4. Market variability — if every buyer wants roughly the same thing → undifferentiated. If buyers have visibly different tastes → only differentiated or concentrated satisfies them. Dr. Alaa: "market variability يعني درجة الاختلافات في السوق" [Lec 3-2 @ 36:16].
  5. Competitor marketing strategies — if every major competitor is running differentiated with multiple variants, you usually have to match or you'll look impoverished and lose share. [Lec 3-2 @ 37:34]

3.10 Socially responsible target marketing

The last sub-topic of targeting (slide 28) asks an ethical question: just because a firm can target a segment, does that mean it should? [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.28 ; Lec 3-2 @ 37:36]

Some segments are vulnerable — they need protection from aggressive or harmful targeting. The two examples on the slide:

  • Children with potentially harmful or controversial products — cigarettes targeted at minors, sugary cereals with cartoon mascots, junk food advertised during kids' programming. Dr. Alaa told the Nike-children-labour story [Lec 3-2 @ 38:08-38:30] — a brand celebrating its global success made a film that accidentally exposed children working in their supply chain, and the brand was hit hard for child-labour abuse.
  • Internet abuses — predatory online targeting, addictive games, dark-pattern paid loops, exploiting psychological weaknesses of teenagers, mining personal data without consent.

Other vulnerable segments often raised in the same discussion: addicts (alcohol, gambling, junk food), the elderly (financial scams, miracle-cure products), people in financial distress (predatory lending). The principle: a marketer is socially responsible not when they refuse to target these groups, but when they protect them from products that would damage their long-term welfare. This is the Societal Marketing orientation from Ch.1a §1.2 in action.

3.11 Differentiation & Positioning — the 3-step build process

Differentiation and positioning are two halves of the same job: differentiation is the real-world change you make to the offer; positioning is where that change lives in the customer's mind.

Deck-verbatim — Product position definition [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.29]: "Product position is the way the product is defined by consumers on important attributes—the place the product occupies in consumers' minds relative to competing products."

Deck-verbatim — Competitive advantage definition [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.31]: "Competitive advantage is an advantage over competitors gained by offering consumers greater value, either through lower prices or by providing more benefits that justify higher prices."

Deck-verbatim — 3-step process heading [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.30]: "Choosing a Differentiation and Positioning Strategy" consists of:

  1. Identifying a set of possible competitive advantages to build a position
  2. Choosing the right competitive advantages
  3. Selecting an overall positioning strategy

Use to understand (not to quote on exam):

  1. Identify a set of possible competitive advantages. List every single way your offer could be different and better — across product, services, channels, people, image. Cast a wide net first; you'll narrow later.
  2. Choose the right competitive advantages. From that long list, pick the one (or two) that actually matter to the customer, that you can deliver consistently, and that competitors cannot easily copy.
  3. Select an overall positioning strategy. Decide the value proposition (more benefit at higher price, or greater benefit at the same/lower price), then write the positioning statement.

Step 1 — the 5 bases of differentiation [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.32]:

Deck-verbatim [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.32]: "Identifying a set of possible competitive advantages to build a position by providing superior value from:"

  • Product differentiation
  • Service differentiation
  • Channel differentiation
  • People differentiation
  • Image differentiation

Use to understand (not to quote on exam):

  • Product differentiation — features, performance, style, durability (Sharp AC's air-purifying tech).
  • Service differentiation — installation, after-sales, warranty. Classic example: Apple's 2010 iPhone-migration-from-Nokia service — they provided the cables and a free in-store device that moved your Nokia contacts/photos/emails onto your new iPhone. [Lec 3-2 @ 50:30]
  • Channel differentiation — store experience, digital integration, distribution reach.
  • People differentiation — staff quality. The attentive waiter vs. the distracted waiter — same restaurant, very different experience [Lec 3-2 @ 51:41].
  • Image differentiation — brand perception, symbols, logos, heritage. Rolex sells image as much as it sells timekeeping.

Step 2 — what makes a "right" advantage: - Important — the customer actually values it. - Distinctive — competitors don't already deliver it. - Communicable — you can explain it in one sentence. - Affordable — the customer can pay for the value it adds. - Profitable — you can deliver it at a margin. - Sustainable — competitors can't easily copy it. Today's advantage can be copied (Skechers copied Crocs; Huawei copied Apple).

Step 3 — value proposition phrasing Dr. Alaa emphasised verbatim: - "Greater value at the same or lower price." — more benefit, same cost. - "Providing more benefit that justifies a higher price." — premium positioning.

Positioning is the mental place. Positioning is the place your brand occupies in the customer's mind relative to competitors. It is not about your product; it is about their perception. Dr. Alaa's phrasing: "ضع نفسك في مكانة ذهنية مميزة" — "put yourself in a distinctive mental place" [Lec 3-1 @ 31:44].

Slide 29 unpacks "the customer's mind" into three specific elements [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.29]: - Perceptions — what the customer thinks about the brand on important attributes (quality, price, reliability). E.g., "BMW = engineered to drive." - Impressions — what the customer remembers from past exposure (ads, packaging, store visits, word of mouth). E.g., an Apple Store as bright, minimal, premium. - Feelings — the emotional response the brand triggers (pride, comfort, excitement, trust, fear). E.g., wearing a Rolex = pride; flying first class = status; using Amazon = convenience.

A purely rational positioning (perceptions only — "we have the lowest price") is fragile; the strongest positioning combines all three (perceptions + impressions + feelings).

Positioning slogans Dr. Alaa cited: - OPPO = "Camera Phone" (camera as the hook) [Lec 3-2 @ 53:45] - Nokia = "Connecting People" [Lec 3-2 @ 53:55] - Toyota in the Gulf = best air-conditioning + durability + fast spare parts [Lec 3-1 @ 56:44] - Ford and Mazda in Egypt — failed to establish a distinctive positioning, which is why they lost share.

The positioning statement [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 s.33] — memorise this template verbatim:

"To (target segment and need), our (brand) is (concept) that (point of difference)."

Worked example using OPPO: "To young photographers who want a phone that is also their main camera, our OPPO is the camera-phone that captures DSLR-quality images in a pocket-sized device."

Core concepts

1. What is a Product? Three levels

The book's exact definition: "Product is anything that can be offered in a market for attention, acquisition, use, or consumption that might satisfy a need or want." [Slide 3] A service is the intangible sibling: "any activity or benefit that one party can offer to another that is essentially intangible and does not result in the ownership of anything." [Slide 3] An experience is what buying the product or service does for the customer (e.g., a Disney holiday, an AppleCare visit) [Slide 3].

Figure 8.1 — Three Levels of Product [Slide 4]:

Level What it really is Example (BlackBerry, from slide)
Core customer value The fundamental benefit that solves the customer's problem. Buyers don't buy products — they buy benefits. "Freedom" and "on-the-go connectivity" — not the device itself.
Actual product The tangible thing the customer takes home. The slide lists: brand name, features, quality level, design, packaging. The physical BlackBerry handset with its keyboard, screen, OS, branding.
Augmented product All the extras around the actual product that add real value: delivery and credit, after-sale service, product support, warranty. Carrier plans, software updates, BlackBerry Enterprise Server, warranty.

Dr. Alaa's framing: "الproduct or service... إيه كان نوع الproduct" — every marketing decision starts by asking what level you compete on. Cheap commodity producers compete on "actual product." Premium brands compete on "augmented product" [Lec 4 @ 1:07:35-1:07:37].

Two more anchor examples Dr. Alaa used in class (NotebookLM-captured) for the three levels:

  • MBA programme — "Business Language" as core value. For an MBA student, the core value isn't the certificate at all — "it's a Change in Mindset and Maturity. They are buying the Business Language that allows them to understand how a corporation functions." The certificate is the actual product (the tangible thing you walk away with). The augmented product is the alumni network, career placement, and the lifelong upgrade of business frameworks you'll keep getting. [Lec 4 @ ~18:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]
  • Netflix — Value Innovation by redefining the product. Netflix didn't sell "content" — it redefined the product from a physical outing (cinema trip) to a convenience-based service. They increased the benefit of convenience while slashing the temporal cost (no travel, no parking, no waiting). This is the Value = Benefit / Cost equation applied to product design — a textbook Value Innovation that disrupted an entire industry by attacking the customer's hidden time cost. [Lec 4 @ ~03:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]

2. Product and Service Classifications

2-pre. Needs, Wants, Demand — and market fragmentation

Before the formal "consumer vs industrial" split, Dr. Alaa unpacks the three concepts that drive why a product exists in the first place [Lec 4 @ ~05:00-08:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

  • Need — a biological imperative. Hunger. Thirst. Shelter. You can't market these into existence; they're hard-wired.
  • Want — a culturally or market-shaped form the need takes. "I'm hungry" is a need; "I want a crunchy chicken nugget" is a want.
  • Demand — a want backed by purchasing power. Wanting a Mercedes is a want; being able to pay for it turns it into demand.

The marketer's job is to create new wants out of old needs — and the tool for doing that is market fragmentation (slicing one generic product into many specialised niches). Dr. Alaa's three illustrations:

Generic need Old single product Modern fragmented market (each item = a new "want" the market created)
Hunger (chicken) A whole chicken Strips, wings, nuggets, crunchy boneless air-fryer pieces, breaded, marinated, kids-portion
Hunger (dairy) Basic Roumy / Turki cheese (رومي / تركي) White, light, medium, flavoured (pepper, olive, herbs), varying fat levels, single-serve, kids-shaped
Thirst (tea) Basic black tea Earl Grey, green, white, fruit-infusion, calming/energising blends, ice-tea, RTD bottled

Every cell on the right side of those rows is a new SKU that a marketer convinced a consumer to buy. That's market fragmentation in action — and it's why the "consumer products" classification you're about to learn matters: each fragment can sit on a different point of the convenience↔specialty spectrum.

The first split [Slide 5] is by who buys it and why:

  • Consumer products — bought for personal consumption.
  • Industrial products — bought for further processing or for use in conducting a business.

Dr. Alaa stressed the test: "اي حد بيشتري الgoods لغاية البيع، ده بيطلق عليه industrial" (anyone who buys goods in order to resell, that's called industrial) [Lec 4 @ 27:36-27:42]. So the same physical thing can be a consumer product or an industrial product depending on why the buyer bought it: a tire bought by you for your car = consumer; the same tire bought by a fleet operator = industrial.

2a. Four types of Consumer Products [Slide 6]

Consumer products are classified by how consumers go about buying them — i.e., by the buying effort.

Type Definition (textbook wording) Buying behaviour Dr. Alaa's Egyptian examples
Convenience [Slide 7] Bought frequently, immediately, with minimum comparison and buying effort. Tiny effort. Nearest shop. Auto-pilot. "زي اللي احنا بتشتريها في السوبر ماركت: بسكويت شيبسي، مية، آيس كريم" — Chipsy biscuits, water, ice cream [Lec 4 @ 28:50-29:02]. Plus newspapers, candy, fast food [Slide 7].
Shopping [Slide 8] Customer compares carefully on suitability, quality, price, and style. Multiple stores, online research, family input. "حضرتك هتبدأ تبزل مجهود أكبر شوية... هتنزل تلف، هتروح المولات" — you'll start to exert more effort, go down and walk around, go to the malls [Lec 4 @ 29:48-30:00]. Designer clothes, high-end electronics, medical services [Slide 9]. A Rolex buyer, an Apple iPhone purist. Dr. Alaa: "specialty product منتجات خاصة" [Lec 4 @ 28:16].

Memorise the buying-effort spectrum: Convenience → Shopping → Specialty (increasing effort + brand attachment). Unsought sits outside this spectrum entirely.

2b. Three types of Industrial Products [Slide 11]

Industrial products are classified by the purpose for which they are purchased [Slide 11]:

  1. Materials and parts — raw materials and manufactured materials & parts, usually sold directly to industrial users. Steel coils to a car maker, flour to a bakery, microchips to Apple. [Slide 12]
  2. Capital items — products that aid in the buyer's production or operations. Machinery, factory buildings, forklifts. [Slide 12]
  3. Supplies and services — operating supplies (lubricants, stationery), repair-and-maintenance items, and business services (auditing, legal, consulting). [Slide 12]

2c. Organizations, Persons, Places, Ideas [Slides 13-14]

Kotler stretches "product" beyond physical goods and services to four softer categories that marketing still applies to [Slides 13-14]:

  • Organization marketing — activities to create/maintain/change attitudes & behaviour of target consumers toward an organization (Red Crescent campaigns, AUC's brand image).
  • Person marketing — toward particular people (a politician's campaign, Mohamed Salah's personal brand, a CEO's media presence).
  • Place marketing — toward particular places (Egypt Tourism Authority's "Pyramids" campaign, Dubai's tourism push).
  • Social marketing — using commercial marketing concepts and tools to influence individuals' behaviour to improve their well-being and that of society (anti-smoking campaigns, "Khaleek bel Beit" COVID campaigns, breast-cancer awareness).

3. Individual Product & Service Decisions — Figure 8.2 [Slide 15]

Every product manager works through this five-step chain [Slide 15]:

Product attributes → Branding → Packaging → Labeling → Product support services

3a. Product attributes [Slide 16]

The benefits the product delivers, captured under three headings:

  • Quality [Slide 17] — has two dimensions:
  • Quality level = the level that supports the product's positioning. (You can't sell a Rolls-Royce on Lada-quality and you don't need Rolls quality to sell budget biscuits.)
  • Conformance quality = the product's freedom from defects and consistency in delivering the targeted level of performance. (Whatever level you promised, you must hit every time.)
  • Dr. Alaa's example: a single brand can be sold in three quality levels at three price points. The European importer asks for grade #1; the Egyptian importer asks for grade #4. Same factory, same brand mark, different specs. [Lec 4 @ 1:15:21-1:15:41].
  • Features [Slide 18] — a competitive tool for differentiating from competitors. Each candidate feature is evaluated on customer value vs. cost to the company. (Don't add a feature whose cost exceeds the value the customer would pay for it.)
  • Style and design [Slide 19] — style describes appearance; design contributes to a product's usefulness as well as to its looks. Style = how it looks. Design = how it works and looks. (A car can have flashy style but bad design if doors won't open in winter.)

3b. Branding [Slide 20]

  • Brand = "the name, term, sign, or design — or a combination of these — that identifies the maker or seller of a product or service." [Slide 20]
  • Brand equity = "the differential effect that knowing the brand name has on customer response to the product and its marketing." [Slides 20, 25] It's measured as the brand's ability to capture consumer preference and loyalty [Slide 25]. Two identical bottles of water with different labels — if customers reach for the Nestle Pure Life one and pay 50% more, that price-and-preference gap is the brand equity.

3c. Packaging [Slide 21]

"Designing and producing the container or wrapper for a product." [Slide 21] Packaging is a silent salesperson — it has to attract, describe, and protect.

Dr. Alaa's strong emphasis: "الpackaging ممكن يخليك تشتري الجودة" (packaging can make you buy the quality) [Lec 4 @ 1:12:45]. He named four categories where packaging dominates the purchase decision [Lec 4 @ 1:12:54-1:13:25]:

  • منتجات الأطفال (children's products — colourful, character-led)
  • Perfume / العطور (bottle shape and design carry most of the brand value)
  • Cosmetics — الروج والمسكرة والاستحضارات (lipstick, mascara, etc.)
  • Premium food / cosmetics

His takeaway: "شكل الغلاف والألوان اللي عليه بتعمل إغراءات شديدة جداً" (the wrapper shape and colours create strong temptations) [Lec 4 @ 1:13:01].

3d. Labeling [Slide 21]

"Labels identify the product or brand, describe attributes, and provide promotion." [Slide 21] Dr. Alaa: "الـliboling هو الكلام المكتوب. لازم الماركتير يقرر إيه الكلام اللي هيكتبه. فيه حاجات قانونية معينة لازم تتحط، زي المكونات، زي تاريخ الصلاحية" (labeling is the written text — the marketer decides what to write; legal mandatories include ingredients and expiry date) [Lec 4 @ 1:13:37-1:13:55]. Legal vs commercial content split is important — promotional claims (e.g., "no preservatives", "low calorie") are commercial; ingredients, weight, expiry date, country of origin are legal.

3e. Product support services [Slide 15]

After-sale support, warranty, installation, repair, training. Dr. Alaa's framing: "product support service — خدمات دعم المنتج بعد البيع" (after-sale product support services) [Lec 4 @ 1:14:33-1:14:39].

4. Product Line Decisions [Slides 22-23]

  • Product line = "a group of products that are closely related because they function in a similar manner, are sold to the same customer groups, are marketed through the same types of outlets, or fall within given price ranges." [Slide 22]
  • Product line length = the number of items in the line. [Slide 23]

Two ways to change line length [Slide 23]:

Move Definition Direction
Line filling Adding more items within the present price/quality range of the line. Inward — fill gaps between existing items.
Line stretching — downward Extending the line toward the lower end (cheaper / lower quality) than its current range. Down-market.
Line stretching — upward Extending the line toward the higher end (more expensive / premium) than its current range. Up-market.
Line stretching — both directions Both ends simultaneously. Both.

Dr. Alaa introduced this concept right at the start of Lec 4: "عايز أعرف إزاي بعمل product line filling — يعني إيه filling — لما الشركة تقرر إن هي to add more items within the present range" [Lec 4 @ 00:26-00:46]. Then: "ممكن أعمل حاجة اسمها stretching — يعني تمديد الخط أو مد الخط. Downward stretching... upward stretching... both direction" [Lec 4 @ 01:02-01:42].

Classic Egyptian-market examples to anchor in your head: - Toyota filled its line by adding Camry Hybrid alongside regular Camry — same range, more items = line filling. - Mercedes launched the A-Class to reach younger / lower-income buyers — downward stretching. - Kia added the Sorento and Carnival above the Sportage to reach premium buyers — upward stretching. - A toothpaste brand adding Sensitive + Whitening + Kids variants at similar price points = line filling.

5. Product Mix Decisions [Slide 24]

  • Product mix = "all the products and items that a particular seller offers for sale." [Slide 24]
  • It's evaluated on four dimensions [Slide 24]:

6. Branding Strategy — Building Strong Brands [Slides 25-31]

Figure 8.3 — Major Brand Strategy Decisions [Slide 26], a four-step flow:

Brand positioning → Brand name selection → Brand sponsorship → Brand development

6a. Brand positioning [Slide 26]

Three levels a brand can be positioned on:

  • Attributes — features and specifications (e.g., Tide gets your clothes white).
  • Benefits — what the attribute does for you (e.g., Volvo = safety).
  • Beliefs and values — what the brand stands for in the world (e.g., Patagonia = sustainability, Apple = think different).

Beliefs-and-values is the strongest position because it's hardest for competitors to copy.

6b. Brand name selection — "Desirable qualities" [Slide 27]

A good brand name should be:

  1. Suggest benefits and qualities (e.g., Beautyrest mattresses, Dove soap)
  2. Easy to pronounce, recognize, and remember (short, phonetic)
  3. Distinctive (not generic)
  4. Extendable (works on future products too — Amazon started with books, but the name works for everything)
  5. Translate easily into foreign languages (Chevrolet's "Nova" famously means "doesn't go" in Spanish)
  6. Capable of registration and legal protection

And once selected, the brand must be defended legally — Dr. Alaa raised the counterfeit issue: "العلامات التجارية المقلدة... 70% من المنتجات المقلدة في العالم — الصين" (counterfeit trademarks... 70% of the world's counterfeits — China) [Lec 4 @ 1:31:28-1:31:46]. Companies like Tommy Hilfiger lose serious revenue to fakes, which is exactly why the textbook lists "capable of registration and legal protection" as a brand-name requirement.

6c. Brand sponsorship — four options [Slide 28]

Option What it means Example
Manufacturer's brand The producer's own name on the product. "Nestlé Nido", "Apple iPhone", "Toyota Corolla".
Private brand (store brand) The retailer's own name on a product the retailer doesn't manufacture. Carrefour-branded biscuits, Spinneys-branded juice, Costco's Kirkland line.
Licensed brand A brand name licensed from another company (entertainment, sports, celebrity). Disney-branded children's clothing, Ferrari-branded sunglasses, Mickey Mouse on a bag of pasta.
Co-brand Two established brand names on the same product. Intel-Inside on a Dell laptop, Visa-Mastercard with banks, Apple-Nike Sports Watch.

9b. Four Service Characteristics — Figure 8.5 [Slide 33]

The Big Four. Memorise these — they are textbook exam material.

Characteristic Definition (verbatim from slide) What it means in practice
Intangibility "Services cannot be seen, tasted, felt, heard, or smelled before purchase." You can't try a haircut before paying. Marketers compensate with tangible cues — clean uniforms, professional offices, before/after photos.
Inseparability "Services cannot be separated from their providers." Your barber is part of the haircut. If she's having a bad day, your service is bad. Production and consumption happen at the same time.

Common confusions & key distinctions

If you see… …think about which side it's on
Product vs Service Service = intangible, no ownership transfer [Slide 3]. Product = tangible (or branded experience).
Consumer product vs Industrial product Personal consumption = consumer. Resale / further processing / business use = industrial [Slides 6, 11].
Convenience vs Shopping Convenience = bought frequently, immediately, minimum comparison. Shopping = customer compares carefully on suitability, quality, price, style. [Slides 7-8]
Shopping vs Specialty Shopping = comparison-shopper, will substitute. Specialty = brand-loyal, will travel for the specific brand, won't substitute [Slide 9].
Specialty vs Unsought Specialty = customer actively seeks it out (brand loyalty). Unsought = customer doesn't normally think of buying it (insurance, blood donation) [Slides 9-10].
Quality level vs Conformance quality Quality level = the target (low / medium / high) that fits positioning. Conformance = how consistently you hit that target without defects [Slide 17].
Style vs Design Style = appearance only. Design = appearance + usefulness [Slide 19].
Brand vs Brand equity Brand = the name/sign that identifies the maker. Brand equity = the differential customer response caused by knowing the brand [Slide 20].
Packaging vs Labeling Packaging = the physical wrapper/container. Labeling = the written information on the package (legal + commercial) [Slide 21].
Line filling vs Line stretching Filling = adding items within the current price/quality range. Stretching = extending the range outward (up, down, or both) [Slide 23].
Line stretching down vs up Downward = extending toward cheaper/lower-quality end. Upward = extending toward premium end [Slide 23].
Product mix Width vs Length Width = number of different lines. Length = total number of items across all lines [Slide 24].
Product mix Length vs Depth Length = items across the whole mix. Depth = versions of each item (sizes, formulations) [Slide 24].
Line extension vs Brand extension Line extension = existing brand + existing category (Diet Coke). Brand extension = existing brand + new category (Honda motorbikes) [Slide 29].
Multibrands vs New brands Multibrands = new brand name but existing category (P&G's Tide, Ariel, Bonux). New brands = new brand name AND new category (Toyota → Scion) [Slide 29].
Manufacturer's brand vs Private brand Manufacturer = producer's own name. Private = retailer's own name (Carrefour, Spinneys) [Slide 28].
Licensed brand vs Co-brand Licensed = you rent someone else's name (Disney on kids' clothes). Co-brand = two equal brands on one product (Intel Inside on Dell) [Slide 28].
Service characteristics — 4 of them Intangibility (cannot be sensed before purchase), Inseparability (cannot be separated from provider), Variability (quality depends on who/when/where/how), Perishability (cannot be stored). Memorise IIVP. [Slide 33]

Chapter 10 — Pricing (Kotler Ch.10)

In one sentence

Price is the amount of money charged for a product or service — and crucially, it is the only element in the marketing mix that produces revenue; every other P (product, place, promotion) represents a cost. [Slide 3]

In plain language

The other three Ps cost the company money. The factory costs money. The trucks that move the product to the shelf cost money. The TV ads cost money. Price is the only lever that turns those costs into cash coming back into the firm. Get it wrong and you either leave money on the table (too low) or scare customers away (too high). Get it right and the rest of the marketing plan funds itself.

Where does the "right" price come from? Three different angles, all valid, used in combination: - Customer perceptions of value set the ceiling. Above this price, no one will buy because they don't think the product is worth it. [Slide 4] - Product costs set the floor. Below this price, the company loses money on every unit. [Slide 4] - Other internal and external factors (your marketing strategy, the competitors' prices, the type of market you're in, the economy, the government) live in the band between the floor and the ceiling. [Slide 4]

This is the Price Ceiling / Price Floor framework [Figure 10.1]. If your costs (floor) ever rise above what customers will pay (ceiling), you have no viable pricing zone — exit that market.

Price is a multi-dimensional value sacrifice — not just money

Dr. Alaa's foundational reframe: the "price" the customer pays is the total exchange of value, not just the monetary number on the tag. The customer surrenders four distinct kinds of cost to acquire your product [Lec 5 @ ~00:15-02:10, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

Cost type What the customer gives up Example
Monetary (explicit) Cash, tuition, broker fees, running costs Apartment purchase price, MBA tuition
Time Cost Hours/days/months of life spent searching, comparing, coordinating Months of apartment-hunting visits and broker calls
Effort Cost Physical and logistical energy Driving to multiple sites, carrying things, managing paperwork
Psychological Cost Stress, anxiety, mental burden MBA exam stress; coordinating a major life purchase
Opportunity Cost Foregone alternatives — family time, leisure, other purchases MBA = time NOT spent with kids, NOT on another course, NOT building a business

Strategic "So What?" — For the manager, the imperative is to minimize the customer's implicit costs (Time, Stress, Effort) to justify a higher monetary price. If a brand reduces the searching cost (good shopping experience, clear information) or the stress cost (reliable delivery, easy returns) or the time cost (fast service, single visit), it captures more of the value surplus and can charge more for the same physical good. Netflix's whole proposition is "we cut your temporal cost of going to a cinema" — and the monthly subscription price reflects that captured surplus.

"Take your MBA as another example. Is the price just the tuition? No. You are paying with your nerves, your stress during exams, and your time away from family. Apartment hunting example [Lec 5 @ ~01:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]: buying an apartment is not just the millions of pounds you pay the seller — it's the broker's commission, the location, the months spent calling brokers, visiting sites, meeting agents (Time Cost), the physical and mental energy expended (Effort Cost). Core concepts

1. The three pricing strategies (and how to pick between them)

Strategy Anchor used to set price When it works
Value-based pricing Buyers' perceptions of value — what they think the product is worth Differentiated products with strong brand or features the customer truly values [Slide 6]
Cost-based pricing The cost to make + a standard markup Stable industries, commodity products where competitors have similar costs [Slide 6, 7]
Competition-based pricing Competitors' prices for the same/similar offering Pure competition or oligopoly markets where customers see products as substitutes [Slide 17]

The key contrast on Slide 6: "Value-based pricing is customer driven. Cost-based pricing is product driven." Cost-based asks "what did this cost me to make?" first, then bolts on a margin. Value-based asks "what is this worth to my customer?" first, then engineers cost down to fit.

Dr. Alaa hammered this with the iPhone case [Lec 5 @ 27:18 source B]: a phone selling at $1000 may cost Apple only $100 to manufacture — about 10% of the selling price. The other 90% margin exists because customers attach value to the brand, the OS, the camera, the lifestyle. "المستهلك بيشتري بناء على ال value — customer value — ده ال value-based pricing" [Lec 5 @ 28:42 source B]. Compare a Toyota Corolla at 1.5 million EGP — three times the production cost — still selling, because customers value reliability, resale value, and the Toyota brand [Lec 5 @ 1:12:00 source B].

2. Customer perceptions of value (Slide 5–6)

Customer-oriented pricing means understanding how much value consumers place on the benefits they receive and setting a price that captures that value [Slide 5]. The value is not in the metal or the plastic — it's in what the product does for the customer's life.

Situational utility — the bottle of water case (Temporary Monopoly)

Dr. Alaa's signature case for how context moves the price ceiling [Lec 5 @ ~06:45-07:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

"How much is a bottle of water? Five, seven, maybe ten pounds? But would you pay 150 pounds for it? [Student: Only in the desert!] Or if you are at an Amr Diab concert in the North Coast — you've been there for seven hours, you are thirsty, and you aren't allowed to bring your own water. If they tell you the bottle is 150 or 200 pounds, you will buy it. In that context, the value equals 200 pounds because the situational utility is at its peak."

Strategic principle: situational utility creates a "Temporary Monopoly." For the manager this means pricing should be dynamic. By controlling the environment (an exclusive venue where competitors aren't allowed in) or by identifying urgent / inelastic needs (water in the desert, antibiotics in a hospital, a phone charger at an airport), a firm can legally push the price toward a ceiling that is exponentially higher than the cost-based floor. Same bottle, same cost — but the context changes the perceived value 30× over.

Brand equity decouples price from cost — the 4 luxury value drivers

For premium brands, the customer is not paying for the physical product — they are paying for a bundle of intangibles. Dr. Alaa's Mercedes GLC case [Lec 5 @ ~11:15-12:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

"Look at a Mercedes GLC. It might cost five or six million pounds. Does Mercedes price based on cost or value? A Chinese car might have more features and gadgets for half the price. Why does the customer still want the Mercedes? [Student: For the star! For the prestige.] Exactly. They want the 'star,' the luxury image, the social distinction, and the resale value. The customer knows they can sell it easily later."

The four luxury value drivers for premium-brand pricing:

Driver What the customer is buying Why it justifies the premium
The Star / Logo Symbolism of social status, elite achievement Visible signal to the customer's social circle
Resale Value Security of a liquid asset retaining worth The premium is partly recovered when sold
Luxury Image Psychological satisfaction of prestige Self-perception, emotional return

Harley-Davidson — Psychographic profiling and community-driven loyalty

Harley-Davidson doesn't sell motorcycles — it sells a "Bikers" lifestyle [Lec 5 @ ~13:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

"Harley-Davidson doesn't just sell motorcycles; they sell a 'Bikers' lifestyle. They study their customers' age, income, and profession to understand why they form clubs and ride together on Friday mornings. This community-driven loyalty makes the customer price-insensitive, as they are paying for admission into a specific social hierarchy."

The company has invested decades in profiling exactly who its customer is (the demographics: age, income, occupation; the psychographics: weekend-club affiliation, brotherhood identity). When you buy a Harley, you're buying entry to that tribe. Price sensitivity collapses because the customer can't get the same identity from any cheaper alternative — a Chinese sportbike at one-fifth the price doesn't grant Bikers Club membership.

The same drink (instant coffee, espresso, latte) can sell for 5 LE at a local café (powdered "ceramic" Nescafé, plastic cups, no ambience) or 90+ LE at Starbucks (branded paper cup, sofa, Wi-Fi, music). The customer is paying for atmosphere and trust, not for the raw ingredient [Lec 5 @ ~15:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

"Whether it's a Mercedes or a Starbucks coffee vs. a 'ceramic powder' Nescafé at a local cafe, the customer pays for the atmosphere and trust."

For a manager, building a brand is the ultimate defense against "cost-plus" competition. Brand equity lets the firm dictate the ceiling rather than be trapped by the floor.

3. Company and product costs (Slides 7–13)

Cost-based pricing adds a standard markup to the cost of the product. To use it you must understand the structure of your costs.

Types of costs [Slide 8]: - Fixed costs — do not vary with production or sales level. Examples on slide: rent, heat, interest, executive salaries [Slide 9]. Dr. Alaa: "زي ايجار المكان — سواء انتجت أو ما انتجتش هتدفع الإيجار، هتدفع مرتبات الإدارة" [Lec 5 @ 58:18 source B]. - Variable costs — vary with the level of production. Examples: packaging, raw materials [Slide 10]. - Total costs = fixed + variable for any given output level. - Average cost = total cost ÷ units produced.

Cost behaviour at scale [Slide 11 / Figure 10.3]: inside a fixed-size plant, the short-run average cost (SRAC) curve is U-shaped — costs drop as volume rises (you spread fixed costs over more units), then eventually rise again as the plant hits capacity. Across plants of different sizes, the long-run average cost (LRAC) curve is the envelope of all the SRACs — showing the best cost a firm could achieve if it could redesign its plant for any volume. The bottom of LRAC is the minimum efficient scale.

The experience / learning curve [Slide 12 / Figure 10.4]: as cumulative production rises, average cost falls — not just because fixed costs spread, but because the firm learns (better suppliers, better methods, less waste, better quality). Dr. Alaa's Egyptian example: the tahina-and-halawa market has only ~10–15 producers (Rashidi El-Mizan, El Halawany, and a few others) because incumbents have a learning curve that new entrants cannot easily climb — they know the suppliers, the secret recipes, the quality tricks [Lec 5 @ 51:00 source B]. Halawany's halawa simply tastes different from Rashidi's, because of decades of accumulated process knowledge.

Cost-plus pricing [Slide 14]: take the cost, add a standard markup (the percentage typical for your industry — could be 10%, 15%, 20%, 30%, or 100% depending on the sector). Banks use a "markup fees" rate that has moved between 5 and 10 and back to 5 in recent years in Egypt [Lec 5 @ 56:30 source B]. Advantages: sellers are certain about their costs; prices look similar across the industry so price wars are minimised; consumers see it as fair. Disadvantage: it ignores demand and competitor prices — you might be charging far below what customers would actually pay [Slide 14].

Break-even and target-profit pricing [Slide 15 / Figure 10.5]: Break-even is the volume at which total revenue equals total cost — no profit, no loss. Target-profit pricing extends this: pick the unit price that lets you hit a target profit at an expected volume. Dr. Alaa's Felafel-shop example [Lec 5 @ 59:30 source B]: "the foul/falafel shop knows it must sell 1,000 sandwiches a day to cover its costs. Every sandwich after the thousandth is pure profit. Below 1,000, it's losing money." That's break-even thinking applied to a corner shop.

Unconscious losses — why SMEs misprice

Dr. Alaa: small-shop owners chronically misprice by only counting the variable costs they can see (raw materials, immediate inputs) and forgetting the running fixed costs that consume the firm whether it produces or not [Lec 5 @ ~17:15, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

"How does a small falafel shop price a sandwich? He looks at the bread, the beans, the tahini. He says it costs 5 or 6 pounds, so he sells it for 8. Is this correct? No! He forgot the Running Costs — the rent, the electricity, the labor, the taxes, even the fines from the local municipality!"

When the manager only sees variable costs, the firm operates at an unconscious loss: the books look fine on a per-sandwich basis, but cash silently drains because fixed costs are not being recovered. The break-even framework forces the discipline of counting all costs before setting price.

3b. The Loyalty Ladder — 4 stages from Satisfaction to Partnership

Marketing is an investment in customer psychology. High marketing efficacy transforms a one-time purchaser into a Partner, which grants the company immense pricing power and protection against market volatility [Lec 5 @ ~22:00-25:00, source: NotebookLM transcript].

Dr. Alaa's Four Stages of the Loyalty Ladder:

Stage What the customer does Pricing power for the firm
1. Satisfaction Baseline — the product met the stated requirements. Customer is content but not yet attached. Low — customer will switch if a competitor offers slightly better value.
2. Loyalty Repeated purchases and consistent brand preference. Customer is now a habit. Medium — switching cost is now psychological; some price premium tolerable.
3. Advocacy Customer actively defends the brand against critics and recommends it to friends and colleagues. High — customer is now doing marketing work for the brand for free.
4. Partnership Highest level — customer feels a sense of "ownership" and deep connection with the brand. Maximum — customer is price-insensitive.

Strategic "So What?" — A "Partner" is no longer a transactional buyer. They become an unpaid sales force: defending the brand on social media, bringing in new customers by word-of-mouth, tolerating price increases without complaint, and forgiving the occasional service slip. Price sensitivity collapses once the customer reaches Partnership, which is the entire point of investing in identity-driven branding.

Skipping stages — the Blue Blue Asia / Sokhna case in this lens [Lec 5 @ ~25:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]: when a developer delivers a project six months ahead of schedule (as in the Sokhna example), they skip the "Satisfaction" stage entirely and move the customer directly to Partner. The buyer never goes through the doubt phase — they receive immediate evidence that the firm exceeded its promise, and they bring the next batch of buyers themselves (which is exactly what happened: judicial-authority bulk purchase of Phase 1).

This is also why Dr. Alaa's Asian-themed compound (above) didn't just sell apartments — the identity turned residents into Partners who advertised the place for free. Partner-stage customers reduce the firm's promotion costs and lift its pricing ceiling simultaneously.

4. Other internal and external considerations

Type of market you're competing in [Slide 17]: - Pure competition — many sellers, identical product (commodities). Price is set by the market; you can't deviate. Dr. Alaa's banana-seller example [Lec 5 @ 57:14 source B]: every grocer sells the same kilo of bananas — "كيلو الموز معروف سعره — مقدرش هرفع الرينج ده بشكل كبير. هنا الأسعار بيحكمها المنافسة في السوق". Try to price above the going rate and no one buys. - Monopolistic competition — many sellers, differentiated products. Some pricing flexibility because customers see brands as different (restaurants, clothing, branded foods). - Oligopoly — few sellers, watching each other closely (telecom, airlines, automakers). - Pure monopoly — one seller, no substitutes (a regulated utility, a government-controlled service).

Price–demand relationship [Slide 18]: normally price up → demand down (inverse / negative relationship). Exception: for prestige (luxury) goods, higher price can equal higher demand because customers read the price as a quality signal — "if it costs more, it must be better." Sony TVs, Mercedes cars, Rolex watches — when price drops, buyers get suspicious.

Price elasticity of demand [Slide 19 / Figure 10.6]: - Inelastic demand — a big change in price produces only a small change in quantity (steep curve). Necessities, monopolies, prestige goods. - Elastic demand — a small change in price produces a big change in quantity (shallow curve). Luxuries with substitutes, undifferentiated commodities.

Other external factors [Slide 20]: Economic conditions (inflation, recession), Reseller's response to price (will distributors push it?), Government (regulations, price ceilings, taxes), Social concerns (price gouging, ethical pricing).

5. New-product pricing strategies (Slides 21–23)

Two opposite strategies for the very first price of a new product:

Market-skimming pricing [Slide 22]: launch at a high initial price to "skim" maximum revenue from the layer of customers who will pay the most. Conditions that must hold: - Product quality and image must support the high price. - Buyers must want the product at that price. - Producing in small volume must not destroy the high-price advantage. - Competitors must not be able to enter easily.

Classic example: Apple iPhone launches — premium price first year, gradually lowered as later iPhones launch. Dr. Alaa: "الناس بيشتروا منتج فاخر — السيارات الفخمة، النظرات، الساعات الفخمة، الملابس الفخمة... ده quality — quality تاع المرسيدس عالية، حتى النهاردة بتلاقي عربية مرسيدس موديل 80 أو 90 ماشية في الشارع" [Lec 5 @ 1:10:30 source B]. Customers tolerate the high price because durability, image, and resale value protect their investment.

Market-penetration pricing [Slide 23]: launch at a low initial price to penetrate the market quickly and deeply — grab market share fast. Conditions: - Price-sensitive market. - Production and distribution costs fall as sales grow (so the low price becomes sustainable). - Low prices must keep competitors out.

Dr. Alaa's Chinese-car case [Lec 5 @ 1:13:23 source B]: Chinese auto brands entering Egypt priced their flagships at 3–5 million EGP — much lower than Mercedes — to penetrate fast. But the brand is not yet known, so customers hesitate. "براندات الصينية تاع العربيات الغالية دي لسه مش مشهورة — لما يكبرش يرفع سعر البراند" — once the brand grows, they can raise prices and migrate toward skimming.

6. Product-mix pricing strategies (Slides 24–27)

Five strategies for pricing a family of related products [Slide 24]:

Product-line pricing [Slide 25]: set price steps between products in the line based on cost differences, customer perception of features, and competitor prices. Example: Toyota's line — Yaris (entry), Corolla (mid), Camry, Land Cruiser (top). The customer sees one ladder of prices that helps them self-select [Lec 5 @ 1:22:34 source B]. Cross-subsidy logic — sometimes the firm deliberately loses margin on one line item to gain sales on another: a juice producer may price Orange juice at a loss (high-volume staple, attracts buyers to the shelf) to drive higher-margin Mango sales within the same line [Lec 5 @ ~38:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]. The line is priced as a portfolio, not as isolated SKUs.

Optional-product pricing [Slide 25]: price the main product and price optional/accessory products separately. TV with optional wall mount — the TV alone is one price, the mount adds to it [Lec 5 @ 1:23:02 source B]. Car with optional GPS (built-in navigation upgrade) [Lec 5 @ ~38:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]. Mobile phone with optional extended warranty year [Lec 5 @ 1:23:14 source B] — the warranty is an optional add-on the customer can take or leave; the base phone is priced without it. Sunroof, leather seats, sound system on a car all follow the same logic.

Captive-product pricing [Slide 26]: products that must be used with the main product. The main product can be cheap; the captive consumables make the profit. Classic examples: razor handles cheap, blades expensive; printer cheap, ink cartridges expensive; Nespresso machine cheap, pods locked-in. For services this is called two-part pricing — a fixed fee + a variable usage fee (mobile subscription monthly fee + per-call charges; gym membership + per-class fee).

By-product pricing [Slide 27]: by-products are produced as a side-effect of the main product and have little or no value on their own. Producers will accept any price that covers storage and delivery to avoid disposal costs. Examples: - Tar / asphalt from petroleum refining — pricing it just above storage cost lets refineries clear inventory. - Sawdust from furniture shops [Lec 5 @ ~37:00, source: NotebookLM transcript] — sold to chicken farms (bedding) and bakeries (combustion); turns disposal cost into modest revenue. - Fabric scraps from clothing factories [Lec 5 @ ~37:00, source: NotebookLM transcript] — sold to mattress stuffers and rag merchants; same logic at micro-scale.

The strategic principle: any price above the net storage + removal cost is better than disposal, because the by-product would otherwise consume warehouse space and disposal fees. Egyptian SME workshops survive partly on this by-product revenue stream.

Product-bundle pricing [Slide 27]: combine several products at a reduced price relative to buying each individually. Classic examples: - McDonald's combo meal — burger + fries + drink cheaper than the three separately. - Shampoo + Conditioner combo packs [Lec 5 @ ~38:00, source: NotebookLM transcript] — the supermarket twin-pack costs less than buying both bottles individually; the manufacturer trades unit margin for bigger basket size and category capture. - Cable TV bundles — TV + internet + phone packaged at a discount.

The goal is to push customers toward higher total spend by making the bundle look like a deal — and to make it harder for a competitor to peel off any one item, because the customer has already committed to the bundle.

In one sentence

Promotion (a.k.a. Marketing Communications) is the fourth P of the marketing mix — the specific blend of advertising, sales promotion, public relations, personal selling, and direct/digital marketing that a company uses to persuasively communicate customer value and build customer relationships. When all five tools are coordinated to deliver one clear, consistent, compelling brand message, the result is Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC) [Slide 3, 11].

In plain language

Think of the marketing mix as 4 things you decide about your offer: the Product (what you sell), the Price (what you charge), the Place (how it reaches the buyer), and the Promotion (how you tell the world about it). This chapter is about that last one. Dr. Alaa says the modern word for Promotion is Communication — because today it isn't just "we shout, you listen"; it's a two-way conversation between brand and customer [Lec 4 @ 11:43–12:11, Lec_4_B.md].

The chapter sits on five big ideas:

  1. The Promotion Mix has five tools. Advertising, Sales Promotion, Public Relations (PR), Personal Selling, and Direct/Digital Marketing. Each tool has different strengths — TV ads reach millions but feel impersonal; a sales rep can answer questions but is expensive [Slides 3-8].
  2. The communications landscape has shifted. Consumers are better informed, mass-media is dying, more communication is targeted and digital, and customers are now content-creators themselves [Slide 9-10].
  3. IMC = the company coordinates all its communication channels — TV, social, sales reps, PR releases, in-store displays — to deliver one clear, consistent, compelling message about the brand [Slide 11, 12]. If your TV ad says "premium luxury" but your salesperson talks like a discount peddler, the customer is confused and the message is lost.
  4. Communication itself is a process. A sender encodes a message; the message travels through media with some noise; a receiver decodes it; feedback travels back. Marketers must design every element so the customer's decoded version matches what was encoded [Slide 13].
  5. Developing effective marketing communications is a 6-step recipe. Identify the target audience → Define communication objectives → Design the message → Choose the media → Select the message source → Collect feedback [Slides 14-22]. Plus you have to set a budget and shape the mix tactically — push vs pull [Slides 23-28].

2. The New Marketing Communications Landscape [Slide 9]

Why the textbook re-frames Promotion as Communication:

  • Consumers are better informed — they Google before they buy, read reviews, watch YouTube unboxings.
  • More communication overall — every brand, every retailer, every influencer is in the customer's feed.
  • Less mass marketing — TV's share of attention is collapsing in favor of niche channels.
  • Changing communications technology — social media, streaming, mobile, AI-driven personalization.

3. Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC) [Slides 11-12]

Textbook definition (memorize verbatim): "Integrated marketing communications is the integration by the company of its communication channels to deliver a clear, consistent, and compelling message about the organization and its brands." [Slide 11]

The diagram on Slide 12 shows the five promotion tools (Advertising / Sales Promotion / Public Relations / Personal Selling / Direct Marketing) all arrowed toward a single red box in the centre that reads "Consistent, clear, and compelling company and brand messages". The teaching point: if any one tool sends a different message, the whole communications system loses credibility [Slide 12].

4. The Communication Process Model (9 elements) [Slide 13]

A marketer cannot design good communication without understanding how communication works. The Kotler diagram has 9 elements grouped into two overlapping circles ("Sender's field of experience" overlapping "Receiver's field of experience"):

  1. Sender — the party communicating (the company/brand).
  2. Encoding — the process of putting thought into symbolic form (words, pictures, jingles).
  3. Message — the actual set of symbols transmitted.
  4. Media — the communication channel through which the message travels (TV, magazine, Instagram, billboard, sales rep).
  5. Decoding — the receiver assigning meaning to the encoded symbols.
  6. Receiver — the consumer who reads/sees the message.
  7. Response — the reactions of the receiver after exposure.
  8. Feedback — the part of the response the sender can observe.
  9. Noise — unplanned static / distortion during the process that causes the decoded message to differ from what was encoded.

Why the overlap matters: effective communication only happens within the shared field of experience between sender and receiver. A McDonald's "I'm lovin' it" jingle works in markets where English emotional ads resonate; it fails when culture/language don't share that field [Slide 13 — annotation about applying model to McDonald's].

Encoding/Decoding mismatches — two classic cases Dr. Alaa cited in class:

  • Marlboro Man rebrand. Marlboro originally featured a woman in its ads. Men "decoded" the brand as a feminine cigarette and refused to buy it. The brand re-encoded with the "Marlboro Man" cowboy — strong, rugged, masculine — and the brand surged. Same product, different encoding → completely different decoding. The lesson: a sender's intended meaning is irrelevant if the receiver's culture decodes it differently. [Lec 4 @ ~27:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]
  • Dasani (سَني) bottled-water color. Dasani initially launched in dark-colored bottles. Egyptian consumers "decoded" the dark color as "impure" / dirty water, and avoided the product. The brand re-encoded with a lighter, more transparent blue bottle — and trust returned. Color is part of the encoding — a non-verbal signal that consumers decode instantly. [Lec 4 @ ~28:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]

Exam-friendly framing: both cases prove that encoding is incomplete until you confirm the receiver's decoded interpretation matches your intent. A "wrong" decode is not the customer's fault — it's an encoding failure on the brand's side, and the brand must fix it.

5. Six steps in developing effective marketing communications [Slides 14-22]

The textbook recipe — every step is a likely T/F or MCQ on the final.

Step 1 — Identify the target audience. Decide who you're talking to (current users? prospects? decision-makers? influencers within the household?). The audience determines everything downstream.

Step 2 — Determine the communication objectives [Slides 14, the Buyer-Readiness Stages diagram on its companion slide]. The Kotler Buyer-Readiness Stages are the canonical 6-stage funnel a customer travels:

Awareness → Knowledge → Liking → Preference → Conviction → Purchase

The communicator's job is to figure out which stage the target audience is in and design messaging to push them to the next stage. A new brand might focus on Awareness ads; a niche brand that customers like but don't trust enough to buy might focus on Conviction (testimonials, guarantees). [Slide 14 — Figure 11.3 Buyer-Readiness Stages]

A simpler classical model that Slide 15 highlights is the AIDA model: "Get Attention, Hold Interest, Arouse Desire, Obtain Action" [Slide 15].

Key distinction (exam trap): Buyer-Readiness Stages has 6 steps; AIDA has 4. Both describe the same customer journey from "knows nothing" to "buys". Don't confuse them.

Step 3 — Design the message [Slides 15-16]. Two design decisions:

  • What to say (message content) [Slide 16] — the appeal or theme. Three types:
  • Rational appeal — relates to the audience's self-interest (this product saves you money / works better / lasts longer). Used in B2B, durables, financial services.
  • Emotional appeal — stirs positive emotions (love, joy, pride, humor) or negative ones (fear, guilt, shame) to motivate a purchase. Used in cosmetics, soft drinks, luxury, life insurance.
  • Moral appeal — directed at the audience's sense of right and proper. Used in cause campaigns, CSR, environmental messaging.
  • How to say it (message structure and format) [Slide 15] — verbal phrasing, visual layout, music, length, sequence.

Step 4 — Choose the media [Slides 17-21]. Two big categories:

  • Personal communication channels [Slides 17-19] — two or more people communicate directly (face-to-face, phone, mail, email, internet chat). Three sub-types based on who controls the message [Slide 18]:
  • Company-controlled — company salespeople contacting buyers.
  • Independent experts — consultants, reviewers, doctors making recommendations.
  • Word-of-mouth (WOM) — neighbors, friends, family talking about the brand.
  • Personal communication is effective because it allows personal addressing + immediate feedback [Slide 18].
  • Opinion leaders [Slide 19] = people within a reference group who (because of special skills, knowledge, personality, or charisma) exert social influence on others. Today's social-media influencers are the new opinion leaders.
  • Buzz marketing [Slide 19] = cultivating opinion leaders and getting them to spread your message to their community (modern: influencer marketing, brand-ambassador programs).
  • Non-personal communication channels [Slides 20-21] — media that carry messages without personal contact or feedback. Three sub-types:
  • Major media [Slide 21] — print (newspapers, magazines), broadcast (TV, radio), display (billboards, signs, posters), and online media.
  • Atmospheres [Slide 21] — designed environments that create or reinforce buying leanings (the smell of bread in a supermarket, the lighting of a luxury watch boutique, the soundtrack at Apple Store).
  • Events [Slide 21] — staged occurrences that communicate messages to target audiences: press conferences, grand openings, exhibits, public tours, sport sponsorships.

Step 5 — Select the message source [Slide 22]. The message's impact on the audience is affected by how the audience views the communicator — credibility, expertise, trustworthiness, attractiveness, similarity to the audience. Common sources: celebrities, athletes, entertainers, professionals, health-care providers [Slide 22]. A toothpaste ad delivered by a dentist (expert) hits differently than the same script delivered by an actor (entertainer).

Step 6 — Collect feedback (implied step closing the loop in [Slide 13]). Measure recall, attitude, behavior, and sales response; feed the data back into the next campaign cycle.

6. Setting the Total Promotion Budget [Slides 23-27]

Four classical methods, each with a definition and a weakness to memorize:

Method Definition Weakness
Affordable [Slide 24] Sets the budget at whatever the company can afford after other expenses "Ignores the effects of promotion on sales" [Slide 24 verbatim] — promotion is treated as cost, not as investment driving growth
Percentage-of-sales [Slide 25] Sets the budget at a certain percentage of current or forecasted sales (or unit sales price) "Wrongly views sales as the cause rather than the result of promotion" [Slide 25 verbatim] — counter-cyclical (budget shrinks when sales fall, exactly when more promotion is needed)
Competitive-parity [Slide 26] Sets the budget to match competitor outlays Represents industry standards and avoids promotion wars [Slide 26], but assumes competitors know what you should spend — they don't
Objective-and-task [Slide 27] Sets the budget based on what the firm wants to accomplish with promotion. Three sub-steps: (a) define promotion objectives, (b) determine tasks to achieve them, (c) estimate costs [Slide 27] Hardest to apply (requires careful objective-setting and accurate cost estimation), but logically the best because it ties spending to outcomes

Exam-style mnemonic: think of the methods on a "logic ladder" — Affordable (worst, no logic) → Percentage-of-sales (mechanical) → Competitive-parity (copycat) → Objective-and-task (best, goal-driven). The exam will likely ask which is most defensible strategically → answer: objective-and-task.

7. Shaping the Overall Promotion Mix [Slide 28]

Once the budget is set, the company decides how to split it across the 5 tools (Advertising / Sales Promotion / PR / Personal Selling / Direct). Two textbook framings:

Push vs Pull strategy [Slide 28 — Figure 11.4]:

  • Push strategy — the producer "pushes" the product through the channel. Producer → Retailers/Wholesalers → Consumers. Producer focuses on trade promotion (incentives for retailers) and personal selling to channel members; retailers then promote to consumers. Used heavily in B2B and complex industrial goods.
  • Pull strategy — the producer promotes directly to final consumers, creating demand that "pulls" the product through the channel. Producer → Consumers (advertising, consumer promotion) → demand pressures Retailers → Retailers order from Producer. Used by consumer-brand giants (Pepsi, Coca-Cola, P&G) where consumer demand forces retailers to stock the brand.

Most companies use a combination of push and pull [Slide 28 caption]. Pepsi runs a Pepsi Football League (pull-via-PR, building consumer pull) and offers retailers cooler racks + volume discounts (push-via-trade-promotion).

Choosing the mix also depends on the type of product/market: - Consumer goods → heavy advertising + sales promotion (pull-heavy). - B2B / industrial goods → heavy personal selling + sales promotion (push-heavy, because buyers are fewer and more rational). - Stage of the product life cycle — introduction phase wants Awareness (advertising + PR); maturity wants Reminder ads + sales promotion to defend share; decline wants minimal communication.

8. Modern Communications Environment — Digital Identity, AI, and Platform Logic

Dr. Alaa frames the modern communications environment around a single shift: algorithms now build a digital persona of every consumer and predict needs before the consumer voices them [Lec 4 @ ~30:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]. This is the operational meaning of the "Direct & Digital Marketing" tool today.

The Carpet/Curtain example (Dr. Alaa's verbatim class story):

An individual might never mention needing curtains. But because their community (friends on Facebook) is discussing interior design, and their location tracking shows they recently visited a furniture club, the AI predicts they are "finishing an apartment" — and pushes curtain ads to them. The consumer never typed "curtains" anywhere; the system inferred the need from the digital identity.

The lesson: in the modern environment, marketers no longer wait for the customer to search for a product. The digital-identity system predicts the customer's stage in the buyer-readiness funnel and pushes the right communication at the right moment.

Platform Logic — Facebook vs TikTok:

Dimension Facebook TikTok
Targeting basis Community-based — the platform looks at who your friends are, what they like, what they buy, then projects those signals onto you Individualized — the platform looks only at your watch history, dwell time, swipes, and pauses, then builds a unique digital identity per user
Implication for marketers Reach groups via the social graph (target a community, the message diffuses to its members through likes/shares) Reach individuals via the algorithmic feed (the message must match the individual's behavior signature)
Best for Brand awareness within identifiable interest communities; viral PR; family/group purchases Hyper-personalized direct response; impulse purchases; products with strong visual hook

Exam-friendly framing: this is the modern version of Personal vs Non-personal communication channels. Facebook re-creates word-of-mouth at scale through social-graph cues; TikTok re-creates one-to-one marketing through behavioral data. Both fall under Direct & Digital Marketing in the promotion mix, but they target very different units (group vs individual).

Buyer-Readiness: Awareness → Knowledge → Liking → Preference → Conviction → Purchase. Chapter 14 — Place / Managing Marketing Channels (Kotler Ch.14)

Coverage note. The slide deck for this chapter (product _PPT_C14, 21 slides) covers sections 12.1 Supply Chains and the Value Delivery Network, 12.2 The Nature and Importance of Marketing Channels, and 12.3 Channel Behavior and Organization. Sections 12.4–12.10 (Channel Design, Channel Management, Logistics, Retailing, Wholesaling, Agents) appear only in the Topic Outline on slides 2–3 and were not unpacked in class. This notes file mirrors what Dr. Alaa actually taught and what is on the deck. Where I add brief textbook coverage of an outlined-but-not-taught topic for completeness, it is flagged "outline only".

In one sentence

Place (the third "P" of the marketing mix) is everything a company does to get its product from the producer to the final customer — and a marketing channel is the chain of organizations (wholesalers, retailers, distributors) that the producer enlists to make that happen. [Slide 1, 7]

In plain language

You can build the best product (Ch.8), price it perfectly (Ch.10), and shout the loudest about it (Ch.11) — but if the customer can't find it on a shelf, on a website, or in a store nearby, none of that matters. Place is the bridge that closes the gap between "produced" and "purchased." [Slide 8]

There are two ways to think about that bridge:

  • Supply chain = the "make-and-sell" view. Starts from the factory: raw materials → production → distribution → customer. The producer is in the centre.
  • Demand chain = the "sense-and-respond" view. Starts from the customer: what does the target customer need? Then you organize the chain of resources backwards to deliver it. The customer is in the centre. [Slide 5]

In practice firms aim higher than either — a value delivery network in which suppliers, distributors, and customers all partner together to improve the performance of the whole system, not just their own slice. [Slide 6]

Why bother with intermediaries at all? Because they cut your transaction cost dramatically. Dr. Alaa walks through the textbook example: imagine 3 manufacturers each trying to reach 3 customers directly. That is 3 × 3 = 9 separate contacts. Now put a single distributor between them — each manufacturer talks to the distributor (3 contacts) and each customer talks to the distributor (3 contacts) — total 6 contacts. [Slide 9 — Figure 14.1, M × C = 9 vs M + C = 6]

And the savings explode at scale. Dr. Alaa dramatized this in class:

"Think bigger. Imagine 100 manufacturers and 100 customers. Without a distributor, you have 10,000 individual contacts. With a distributor, you have 200. Imagine the time saved!" [Lec 6 @ ~14:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]

That is the mathematical core: distribution efficiency converts a multiplicative problem (M × C) into an additive one (M + C), directly lowering the search cost for the consumer and providing the three classic utilities of Time, Place, and Possession.

Core concepts

1. Supply chain, demand chain, and the value delivery network [Slides 4–6]

  • Upstream partners of a firm = the suppliers that bring in raw materials, components, parts, information, finances, and expertise needed to create the product or service.
  • Downstream partners = the marketing channels / distribution channels that look towards the customer.
  • Supply chain view ("make and sell"): firm-centred. You start with what you can produce and push it forward.
  • Demand chain view ("sense and respond"): customer-centred. You start with what the target customer needs and pull resources backwards to deliver it.
  • Value delivery network: company + suppliers + distributors + customers all linked as partners with a shared interest in the whole system's performance — not just their own profit. [Slide 6]

2. What is a marketing channel? [Slide 7]

Marketing channel (a.k.a. distribution channel) = a set of interdependent organizations that help make a product or service available for use or consumption by the consumer or business user. [Slide 7 — verbatim Kotler definition]

The two italicised words to memorise: - "interdependent" — every channel member's success depends on the others. The producer cannot reach the customer alone; the retailer has nothing to sell without the producer. - "available for use or consumption" — that's the channel's job description in five words.

Egyptian manufacturer examples

Dr. Alaa anchored the discussion with three local manufacturers that rely on wholesalers + retailers to reach final consumers: - Amreya Spinning and Weaving (الإسكندرية للغزل والنسج) - Baniata (بنياتا) - Basco Masr

Each makes products in a factory and must depend on a chain of intermediaries — "the factory might be in the 10th of Ramadan City or Cairo, but the product must be here for you to buy". [Lec 6 @ ~08:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]

Consumer vs Business User — the restaurant test

Dr. Alaa posed a sharp class question to define the buyer side of the channel definition:

"If a restaurant buys napkins and ketchup, are they a consumer? No. They buy to provide a service and generate profit. They are a Business User." [Lec 6 @ ~10:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]

A student then asked whether a restaurant is a product or a service. Dr. Alaa:

"It's both — a package of benefits. The food is the tangible product; the music, air conditioning, and atmosphere are the intangible services." [Lec 6 @ ~11:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]

This is a clean B2B-vs-B2C distinction wrapped around a product-plus-service framing — exam-grade content.

3. How channel members add value [Slide 8]

Channel members do three things the producer cannot easily do alone:

  1. Transform the assortment. A producer makes a narrow range in huge quantities; a customer wants a broad range in tiny quantities. The channel members break bulk and re-bundle.
  2. Match supply and demand. Channel members buffer mismatches between how much the producer makes and how much customers want.
  3. Bridge the gaps of time, place, and possession between the goods and those who would use them. [Slide 8]

Dr. Alaa's class example for this matching role: "a supermarket in a popular [working-class] neighbourhood will stock cheap brands, small sizes, and reasonably priced brands; a supermarket in an upscale neighbourhood will not stock the same things its colleague in the popular area does." The same chain (Carrefour, Spinneys) carries different assortments per branch because the channel matches the assortment to the local segment. [Lec 6 @ 20:00 — verbatim Arabic in Lec_6_B; Lec 6 @ 20:02 in Lec_6 source A]

4. Channel functions [Slide 10]

Channel members perform 8 functions, all of which would otherwise fall on the producer:

Function What the channel does
Information Gathers and distributes market research / intelligence about buyers, competitors, conditions.
Promotion Develops and spreads persuasive communication about the offer (a retailer's flyer, a distributor's catalogue).
Contact Finds and communicates with prospective buyers — the channel knows the local customer the producer cannot reach.
Matching Shapes and fits the offer to the buyer (assembling, sorting, packaging, custom configuration).
Negotiation Reaches agreements on price and terms so ownership can transfer.
Physical distribution Transports and stores goods.
Financing Acquires and uses funds to cover the channel work (carries inventory, extends credit).
Risk taking Assumes the financial risk of carrying out the channel work (unsold inventory, returns).

[Slide 10]

Dr. Alaa's vivid Risk-Taking examples [Lec 6 @ ~17:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]: - The Fakihani / fruit seller (الفاكهاني) — risks spoilage on perishable produce. If 200 kg of strawberries don't sell today, the fakihani eats that loss, not the farmer or the wholesaler upstream. The channel intermediary is paid (via margin) to absorb that spoilage risk the manufacturer would otherwise face. - The Pharmacy — must manage the return of expired medicines to the producer. The pharmacy carries the inventory risk for the entire shelf-life of the medicine; only on expiry does it negotiate a return-and-replace agreement with the wholesaler/producer. Until then, expired/unsold stock is the pharmacy's loss.

Same logic, two industries: the closer the intermediary sits to the final customer, the more inventory + spoilage risk it absorbs — and that absorbed risk is part of what the producer "buys" by letting the intermediary into the channel.

5. Channel levels (number of intermediaries) [Slide 11 — Figure 14.2; Slide 12]

A channel level = each layer of marketing intermediaries that brings the product closer to the final buyer. The count is the number of intermediaries (the producer and the final customer are NOT counted as levels).

Consumer channels (Figure 14.2 panel A): | Channel | Path | Levels | Examples | |---|---|---|---| | Channel 1 | Producer → Consumer | Direct (0 levels) | Avon doorbell sales, Dell direct, Apple's own stores, a farmer selling at the gate | | Channel 2 | Producer → Retailer → Consumer | 1 level | A clothing brand selling through a department-store chain | | Channel 3 | Producer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer | 2 levels | Almarai → wholesaler → small grocer → you |

Business channels (Figure 14.2 panel B): same logic with industrial intermediaries | Channel | Path | Levels | |---|---|---| | Channel 1 | Producer → Business customer | Direct | | Channel 2 | Producer → Business distributor → Business customer | 1 level | | Channel 3 | Producer → Manufacturer's reps / sales branch → Business distributor → Business customer | 2 levels |

[Slide 11 — Figure 14.2]

What flows along the channel? Slide 12 lists five flows: - Physical flow of products - Flow of ownership (legal title moves) - Payment flow (money moves the other direction) - Information flow (both directions) - Promotion flow (downstream toward the customer) [Slide 12]

How do you choose how many levels to use? Dr. Alaa walked through the rule in class:

"It depends on the physical perishability of the product. Does the product have a long shelf-life or a short one? If the shelf-life is short, you must shorten the channel. If the shelf-life is long, you can lengthen the channel." [Lec 6 @ 50:30–50:55]

Same logic for unit value: high-value, low-volume items (luxury watches, custom industrial equipment) prefer shorter channels for control; low-value, high-volume items (sweets, soft drinks) tolerate longer channels for reach. [Lec 6 @ 51:00–52:00]

6. Channel behavior — conflict and cooperation [Slide 13]

A channel is a partnership — every member plays a specialised role for the common good of the whole channel. But because each member is also an independent business chasing its own profit, channel conflict is inevitable.

Channel conflict = disagreement over goals, roles, and rewards by channel members. [Slide 13 — verbatim]

Two flavours: - Horizontal conflict — between firms at the same level of the channel (two retailers fighting over the same territory; two wholesalers chasing the same big retailer's business). - Vertical conflict — between firms at different levels (producer vs retailer, wholesaler vs producer).

Dr. Alaa's Carrefour-vs-Almarai (المراعي) class example

This is the case to remember for the exam — Dr. Alaa spent ~3 minutes on it. [Lec 6 @ 59:53–1:01:13; same content Lec_6_B @ 57:02–1:01:14]

  • Setup: Almarai (المراعي) is a large Saudi-Egyptian dairy producer. Carrefour is a big multinational retailer.
  • The conflict — two power sources collide:
  • Carrefour wielded Market-Access / Size Power — using its huge retail footprint and shelf-access leverage to dictate terms (shelf fees, payment timing, pricing margins) to suppliers. "Carrefour wanted to use its size to dictate prices." [Lec 6 @ ~18:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]
  • Almarai wielded Brand Power — its strong consumer brand pull let it refuse those terms. "Almarai used its brand power to say no." [Lec 6 @ ~18:00, source: NotebookLM transcript] Verbatim Whisper line: "Almarai sees itself as a big company; it would not let Carrefour dictate terms — I am the one who should be dictating terms to you." [Lec 6 @ 1:00:30]
  • The outcome: Almarai walked out. "All Carrefour branches no longer carry Almarai products." [Lec 6 @ 1:00:44]
  • What it illustrates: a vertical conflict between two channel partners with different sources of power (size vs brand). The lesson — channel power can come from either market access (retailer size) or brand pull (producer strength); when both sides have power, conflict is unresolvable and the channel breaks. Other small/medium retailers still carry Almarai — so Almarai swapped one channel partner for many.

7. How channels are organised — VMS, HMS, Multichannel [Slides 14–20]

7a. Conventional distribution channel [Slide 14]

"One or more independent producers, wholesalers, and retailers. Each seeks to maximise its own profits, and there is little control over the other members and no formal means for assigning roles and resolving conflict."

Plain language: nobody is in charge. Every member optimises for itself. Conflict is constant.

7b. Vertical Marketing System (VMS) [Slide 15]

"VMSs provide channel leadership and consist of producers, wholesalers, and retailers acting as a unified system." [Slide 15 — verbatim]

Plain language: somebody IS in charge. The channel acts as one team. Comes in three forms — Dr. Alaa emphasised this triplet ("VMS comes in three forms" [Lec 6 @ 1:05:12]):

# Type Mechanism of control Examples
1 Corporate VMS Single ownership. One company owns successive stages of production AND distribution. Apple owns its retail stores; Zara owns its retail chain; an oil major owning refineries + petrol stations. Egyptian examples Dr. Alaa cited: Diina Farms (دينا فارمز) — owns the farm AND the retail outlets selling its dairy/poultry. Al-Asdeqaa (الأصدقاء) — similar farm-to-shelf ownership integration. [Slide 16; Lec 6 @ ~19:30, source: NotebookLM transcript]
2 Contractual VMS Independent firms at different levels join by contract. Most common form is the franchise. Sub-forms: manufacturer-sponsored retailer franchise (e.g., Ford dealerships), manufacturer-sponsored wholesaler franchise (Coca-Cola bottling), service-firm-sponsored retailer franchise (McDonald's, KFC). McDonald's, KFC, Cilantro, Carrefour's franchise branches in some markets; Ibn Sina (ابن سينا) — major pharmaceutical distributor; its contracts with hundreds of pharmacies create unified distribution power. [Lec 6 @ ~20:00, source: NotebookLM transcript] Orange / Vodafone service points (مراكز خدمة عملاء أورنج وفودافون) — service-firm-sponsored retailer franchise. [Lec 6 @ ~20:30, source: NotebookLM transcript] Plus the "صيدلية خليل" small-pharmacy chain affiliation. [Slide 17; Lec 6 @ 1:08:38–1:13:09]

[Slide 18; Lec 6 @ 1:14:46–1:15:46]

7c. Horizontal Marketing System (HMS) [Slide 19]

"Two or more companies at one level join together to follow a new marketing opportunity. Companies combine financial, production, or marketing resources to accomplish more than any one company could alone."

Plain language: peer firms at the same channel level team up. Two airlines code-sharing routes. Two banks sharing ATMs. Two grocery chains co-buying a category to get a volume discount.

7d. Multichannel / hybrid distribution [Slides 20–21; Slide 21 — Figure 14.4]

"A single firm sets up two or more marketing channels to reach one or more customer segments."

Plain language: the producer uses several channels in parallel to reach different customer segments. Figure 14.4 (slide 21) is the canonical picture — a Producer pushing through: - a direct channel (catalogues, telephone, internet) → Consumer segment 1 - a retailer channel → Consumer segment 2 - a distributor → dealer channel → Business segment 1 - a direct sales force → Business segment 2

So the same producer simultaneously sells direct online to small consumers AND through retailers to consumer households AND through distributors to small businesses AND via key-account sales reps to big enterprises. [Slide 21 — Figure 14.4]

Dr. Alaa's working example — HP / Dell: a single tech producer reaches segments through four parallel paths [Lec 6 @ ~40:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]: - Direct sales (web / sales force) → tech-savvy individuals + large business users that need bulk customised orders. - Retailers — B.Tech (Egyptian electronics retail chain) and Carrefour → general individual consumers who want physical trial and immediate possession. - Distributors / dealers → smaller specialised businesses or geographically remote markets. - Key-account direct sales reps → enterprise customers needing tailored configuration.

One producer, four simultaneous channels — each one matched to a specific segment's buying behavior. This is the textbook definition of hybrid distribution.

8. Channel design decisions (outline only — section 12.4, not unpacked in the deck)

The textbook process: (1) analyse consumer needs, (2) set channel objectives, (3) identify major alternatives (types of intermediaries; number of intermediaries — intensive / selective / exclusive distribution; responsibilities of channel members), (4) evaluate alternatives (economic / control / adaptive criteria). The three "number of intermediaries" strategies are worth memorising even though they were not on this deck — they are classic test-bank material: - Intensive distribution — stock the product in as many outlets as possible (Coca-Cola, candy, cigarettes — "available everywhere"). - Selective distribution — use more than one but fewer than all intermediaries (mid-tier consumer electronics, mid-tier appliances). - Exclusive distribution — only one intermediary per territory carries the brand (luxury watches, prestige cars).

9. Channel management decisions (outline only — section 12.5)

Textbook stages: (1) selecting channel members, (2) managing and motivating them, (3) periodically evaluating their performance.

10. Marketing logistics & supply-chain management (outline only — section 12.6)

Textbook definition: physical distribution / logistics = planning, implementing, and controlling the physical flow of materials, final goods, and related information from points of origin to points of consumption to meet customer requirements at a profit. Major logistics functions: warehousing, inventory management, transportation, and logistics information. Third-party logistics (3PL) = outsourcing logistics to specialists (e.g., DHL, Aramex, Maersk Line in maritime shipping).

11. Retailing, wholesaling, agents (outline only — sections 12.7–12.10)

Not unpacked on this deck. The two definitions to keep in your back pocket: - Retailer = an intermediary that sells primarily to final consumers for personal, non-business use. - Wholesaler = an intermediary that sells primarily to other businesses for resale or business use. Dr. Alaa's class example: in a popular-area supermarket, "he sells at wholesale price anyway — when the supermarket buys at 450 EGP… they sell the item at a wholesale price" — illustrating how the retailer/wholesaler distinction blurs in everyday Egyptian markets. [Lec 6 @ 40:36–41:18]

12. Choosing channel length — the three deciding factors

How does a manager actually decide whether to use a 0-level, 1-level, 2-level, or 3-level channel? Dr. Alaa wrapped the lecture by naming three explicit factors that drive that choice [Lec 6 @ ~50:00, source: NotebookLM transcript]:

  1. Perishability — physical shelf-life dictates speed. Fresh milk requires a direct, short channel (producer → retailer → consumer, within a day) because spoilage risk dominates. UHT milk (six-month shelf life) tolerates a longer, more efficient channel with wholesalers in the middle because the time gap is not destructive. Same logic for fresh vegetables/fruit vs canned goods, fresh bread vs long-life biscuits.
  2. Flow of Ownership (margin economics) — each intermediary added to the channel takes a margin. The manager must balance that margin cost against the value-add the intermediary brings (reach, expertise, financing, risk-absorption). If the intermediary's service is worth less than its margin, cut it out and go shorter; if its services are worth more than its margin, keep it.
  3. Efficiency (bridging time + place gaps) — the channel exists to span the gap between where/when the product is made and where/when the customer wants it. The longer that gap (geographic distance, mismatched timing), the more channel layers it takes to close it efficiently. A channel of 0 levels works when producer = customer in time and place; longer channels become rational the bigger that separation grows.

These three sit inside the broader textbook decision: every additional level adds reach but adds cost and weakens control. Direct = max control, max cost; long = max reach, min control. The manager's job is matching channel length to product characteristics + customer needs + cost structure.

Common confusions & key distinctions

Confused pair Quick rule
Supply chain vs Demand chain Supply chain = factory-out, make and sell. Demand chain = customer-in, sense and respond.
Marketing channel vs Supply chain A marketing/distribution channel is just the downstream half (producer → final customer). A supply chain is the whole thing (raw materials → final customer).
Channel level count Count intermediaries only. Producer and final customer are NOT levels. Direct = 0 levels.
Conventional channel vs VMS Conventional = nobody is in charge, each maximises own profit, constant conflict. VMS = somebody IS in charge, channel acts as a unified system.
Corporate vs Contractual vs Administered VMS Corporate = one owner. Contractual = binding contracts among independents (franchise). Administered = neither — leadership by size/power alone.
Vertical conflict vs Horizontal conflict Vertical = between channel levels (producer ↔ retailer). Horizontal = between firms at the same level (retailer ↔ retailer).
HMS vs VMS HMS = same-level partnership (peer-to-peer). VMS = cross-level partnership (top-to-bottom unified system).
Multichannel vs Hybrid channels Same thing — Kotler uses both names interchangeably for "one producer, several parallel channels to different segments."
Intensive vs Selective vs Exclusive distribution Intensive = everywhere (Coke). Selective = some, not all (mid-tier electronics). Exclusive = one per territory (luxury).
Retailer vs Wholesaler Retailer sells to final consumers. Wholesaler sells to other businesses (for resale or business use).

TRUE. The Societal Marketing Concept balances THREE interests: (1) customer wants (short-term), (2) company profits (medium-term), and (3) society's long-run welfare. 8. Building up a good corporate image is the primary goal of Sales Promotion.

FALSE. Building good corporate image is the goal of Public Relations (PR), not Sales Promotion. Sales Promotion = short-term incentives to encourage purchase (coupons, discounts, contests). 9. Intensive distribution means selling a product through only one outlet per geographic area, such as a single exclusive dealer.

FALSE. That describes Exclusive distribution (BMW, Rolex). Intensive distribution = sell EVERYWHERE possible (Coca-Cola in every kiosk, Pepsi in every store). [Slide: Kotler Ch.14 — Distribution Intensity]

TRUE. Textbook definition. If perceived value ≥ expectations → satisfaction (or delight). If perceived value < expectations → dissatisfaction. The four customer-satisfaction drivers Dr. Alaa emphasized: (a) cost of obtaining, (b) expectations, (c) cost of competing products, (d) cost of the lost experience. (2) Ch.8 Brand-equity drift within the youth segment — Vodafone is now associated with parents, a positioning erosion identical to Cilantro's pattern but on a segment-specific axis. (3) Ch.10 Pricing trap — Kotler explicitly warns against using price as the lever to fix a non-price problem; this is what discount campaigns are doing here. (4) Ch.11 Promotion mismatch — mass-media IMC channels (TV, FM radio) do not reach TikTok-native youth; the medium itself signals "this isn't for you." Orange's "Yalla Shabab" sub-brand demonstrates the correct competitive answer. [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 + Ch.8 + Ch.10 + Ch.11]

(c) Solutions across the marketing mix. (~310 words)

Model:

STP first (Ch.7): Switch from undifferentiated to differentiated targeting. Re-segment the prepaid base by demographic (age) + psychographic (lifestyle) + behavioral (app usage). Define a distinct youth segment (16–25, social-heavy, multi-app, students or early-career, group-plan-likely). Create a youth-only sub-brand — call it "Vodafone Generation" or "Vodafone Shabab" — visibly different from the mainstream Vodafone red. New positioning statement: "To Egyptian young people who live on TikTok, Instagram, and WhatsApp, Vodafone Generation is the only network that bundles social + entertainment + groups at a price that fits a student's budget." [Slide: Kotler Ch.7 — STP + Differentiated Targeting + Positioning Statement]

Product (Ch.8): Build a sub-brand product line specifically for youth — bundles of unlimited TikTok/Instagram/Snapchat data + WhatsApp + free group voice + low-price voice/SMS. Use product-line filling within the prepaid range (do not stretch into postpaid). Brand-name selection is critical here — Dr. Alaa's verbatim emphasis: distinct sub-brand name signals to youth that "this is for you." Don't just rename existing bundles; build new ones from the segment's needs. [Slide: Kotler Ch.8 — Branding + Line Decisions] [Lec 4 @ 1:11:06]

Price (Ch.10): Stop the discount spiral on the mainstream brand. Use value-based + segmented pricing for the sub-brand: 15–20% lower than mainstream prepaid, but justified by a distinct bundle. The comparison point becomes Orange's "Yalla Shabab," not Vodafone's mainstream — neutralizing the cannibalization risk.

Place (Ch.14): Selective distribution in youth-facing locations — co-brand corners in malls near schools/universities, on-campus kiosks, partner retailers (e.g., Battery + Tea coffeeshops). Reduce reliance on mainstream Vodafone stores that signal "parents' network." Maintain administered VMS with retail partners.

Promotion (Ch.11): TikTok-first IMC, micro-influencer led. PR: launch event with youth artists. Sales Promotion: free-trial week, refer-a-friend bonus, gamified loyalty. Direct & Digital: WhatsApp Business loyalty channel. Personal Selling: campus brand ambassadors. AVOID mass-TV (off-brand for sub-target). Apply the 6-step IMC process rigorously: identify (youth), objectives (move from awareness → preference → switch from Orange), message (rational + emotional), media (TikTok primary), source (influencers > corporate), feedback (NPS + switch rate). Case 4 — El-Cairo Footwear — Distribution Mismatch (NEW problem case)

Full scenario (~210 words):

El-Cairo Footwear is a family-owned Egyptian shoe manufacturer founded in Shoubra in 1985. The company makes ~300,000 pairs/year of leather shoes at a modern factory using imported Italian-style designs. Product quality is genuinely good — independent industry reviewers rate the leather and construction comparable to mid-tier Spanish brands (e.g., Pikolinos) and far above the typical low-tier Egyptian shoe shops.

The owner wants to position El-Cairo Footwear as a premium Egyptian-made brand at 800–1,200 EGP per pair (premium for Egyptian context, mass-affordable globally). However, after 5 years of trying: - Carrefour Egypt, Spinneys, and Seoudi all refused to stock the brand — too expensive for their mass-market positioning. - The brand ends up sold mostly in low-tier traditional shops in Shoubra, Embaba, and Old Cairo at 600–900 EGP — customers in these channels balk at "Egyptian shoe for 900 EGP," so retailers discount further. - A few pairs have made it into upscale boutiques in Zamalek and Maadi, but volume is tiny (~2% of total). - Marketing budget has been ~5M EGP/year, used mostly on Facebook ads — almost no premium-channel work. - The owner has 30M EGP earmarked for a turnaround.

(a) Define the main problem and its nature. (~140 words)

Model: El-Cairo Footwear's main problem is a channel-positioning mismatch of a strategic, multi-lever nature: a genuinely premium product is being sold through mass / low-tier channels, which simultaneously (i) caps perceived value at the lowest channel's tier ("a Shoubra shoe brand can't be premium"), (ii) traps the brand in a price-discount spiral that destroys margin, and (iii) prevents premium customers from ever discovering the brand because they don't shop in those channels. The nature is distribution-intensity mismatch — the company is using intensive distribution (whatever shop will stock it) when it should be using selective distribution (curated upscale boutiques + own brand stores + selective premium department stores). Every other marketing decision is compromised by this mistake. [Slide: Kotler Ch.14 — Distribution Intensity]

(b) Analyze from a marketing point of view. (~160 words)

Model: Three Ch.14 + Ch.7 concepts converge: (1) Distribution-intensity mismatch (Ch.14): the textbook rule is premium goods → selective or exclusive distribution; mass goods → intensive. El-Cairo Footwear violates this by using intensive distribution for a premium product. (2) STP positioning failure (Ch.7): positioning is set in the customer's mind by the channels where they encounter the product. A "premium Egyptian-made shoe" sold next to 200-EGP shoes in Shoubra cannot be perceived as premium, no matter what the box says. (3) Brand-equity erosion (Ch.8): every Shoubra shop sale at a discounted price further commoditizes the brand. The chain is causal: wrong channel → wrong perceived positioning → wrong perceived value → forced discounts → brand commoditization → margin loss. Push vs pull (Ch.11) also contributes: there's no pull strategy to create premium customer demand. [Slide: Kotler Ch.14 + Ch.7 + Ch.8 + Ch.11]

(c) Solutions across the marketing mix. (~310 words)

Model:

Place (Ch.14) — primary lever: Restructure to selective distribution. Specific actions: - Pull out of low-tier shops over 12 months (manage the inventory transition). - Open 6–8 own-brand premium stores in Maadi, Zamalek, City Stars, Mall of Egypt, Mall of Arabia — the customer must encounter El-Cairo in a premium environment. - Negotiate with upscale boutiques (e.g., Diwan-adjacent retail), with selective placement in Spinneys Madinaty / Spinneys Zamalek (premium-tier branches, not mass branches). - Build a direct-to-consumer e-commerce channel with premium delivery experience. - VMS form: Hybrid — Corporate VMS for own stores, Administered VMS for selective premium partners. NOT Contractual (franchise model is wrong for early premium-brand stage). [Slide: Kotler Ch.14 — VMS + Distribution Intensity + Multichannel]

STP (Ch.7) — second lever: Concentrated targeting on urban A/B-class professionals 25–50 who want Egyptian-made premium goods (national-pride segment + premium-quality segment). NOT differentiated (too small to span tiers). New positioning statement: "To Egyptian professionals who want premium handcrafted shoes that rival European brands at half the price, El-Cairo Footwear is the only premium Egyptian-made leather brand."

Product (Ch.8): Strengthen the augmented product — premium packaging (Dr. Alaa's verbatim emphasis), shoe-tree boxes, free annual leather-care service, lifetime resoling. Brand-name redesign — "El-Cairo" works as Egyptian-pride name; reinforce with sub-brands for occasion ("El-Cairo Office," "El-Cairo Weekend"). [Lec 4 @ 1:12:57]

Price (Ch.10): Hold list price at 800–1,200 EGP — do NOT cut. In the new channels, customer-perceived value will rise to meet the price (because the channel anchors the perception). Value-based pricing. Eliminate discount-spiral by enforcing minimum-retail-price agreements with all selective partners.

Promotion (Ch.11): Pull strategy to create demand. Egyptian-heritage IMC narrative (PR + Direct & Digital), premium-magazine advertising (e.g., Business Today Egypt), influencer partnerships with Egyptian professionals/style-leaders. Avoid Facebook mass-market ads — they signal the wrong segment.

The 4Ps (Marketing Mix)

P Definition Key concepts Example
Product Anything offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use, or consumption 3 levels (core/actual/augmented), 4 classes of consumer goods, line decisions, brand equity, PLC iPhone (core: communication; actual: hardware+iOS; augmented: warranty+Genius bar)
Price The amount of money charged for a product or the sum of values customers exchange for benefits Value-based / cost-based / competition-based; skimming vs penetration; product-mix pricing Apple skim → Xiaomi penetrate
Place Channel decisions to make the product available where & when customers want it Channel levels (0/1/2/3), VMS (corporate/contractual/administered), distribution intensity (intensive/selective/exclusive), 3PL Carrefour as administered VMS; Maersk as 3PL
Promotion Communicate the customer value and persuade the target market 5 tools (advertising/SP/PR/personal selling/direct & digital), IMC, 9-element communication model, push vs pull, AIDA Coca-Cola's IMC = TV ad + sponsorship + social + on-shelf

STP — the engine

  1. Segment — divide market into groups by geographic / demographic / psychographic / behavioral bases
  2. Target — choose segments worth pursuing using 5 factors (resources, product variability, PLC stage, market variability, competitor strategy)
  3. Differentiate — find a meaningful, important, distinctive, superior, communicable, preemptive, affordable, profitable advantage
  4. Position — write the positioning statement: "To [target] who [need], our [brand] is [POD] that [benefit]"

SWOT + Distinctive Competency + Success Requirements

  • SWOT = Strengths (internal +) / Weaknesses (internal −) / Opportunities (external +) / Threats (external −)
  • Distinctive Competency = a strength competitors cannot easily replicate (internal). Must MATCH the market's Success Requirements (external) for opportunity to materialize.
  • 3 SWOT diagnostic questions: What are we doing? What are we good at? What's the market demanding?
